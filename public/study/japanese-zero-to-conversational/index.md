Japanese has a reputation as one of the hardest languages for English speakers, and part of that is earned: three writing systems, a verb that always comes last, particles instead of prepositions, and a politeness system built into the grammar. But the sound system is simple, the grammar is very regular once you see the pattern, and there are no genders, no articles, no plurals to agree with, and only two tenses. This guide takes you from zero to a conversational, high-school level: roughly JLPT N4, CEFR A2 heading toward B1. At that level you can introduce yourself, order food, ask for directions, talk about your week and your plans, read simple texts with a dictionary, and hold a slow but real conversation with a patient native speaker.

The order of this guide is the order I teach in the classroom: scripts and sounds first, then polite sentences, then verbs and adjectives, then the plain forms and patterns that make speech sound natural. Every section ends with an exercise. Do them out loud.

![A 16-week timeline with milestones, above a ladder of the five JLPT levels with approximate CEFR levels, kanji counts and vocabulary sizes](study/japanese-zero-to-conversational/jlpt-roadmap.svg "The route: 16 focused weeks, then N5 and N4 on the JLPT ladder")

> **NOTE:** Romaji in this guide uses Hepburn, with long vowels spelled out the way you type them on a keyboard (`ou`, `uu`, `aa`, `ii`): *otousan*, *koohii*, *benkyou*. Textbooks often write the same sounds with macrons: *otōsan*, *kōhī*, *benkyō*. Particles are written as they sound: は is *wa*, を is *o*, へ is *e*. Treat romaji as scaffolding for the first two weeks, then stop leaning on it.

## How long it takes, and how to study

### A realistic timeline

The US Foreign Service Institute puts Japanese in its hardest group for native English speakers (Category IV, alongside Arabic, Mandarin and Korean) and estimates about 88 weeks, around 2,200 classroom hours, to reach *professional working proficiency*. That is diplomat-level Japanese: negotiating, reading newspapers, giving talks. Your goal here is much smaller.

For conversational high-school level, the useful yardstick is the JLPT (Japanese-Language Proficiency Test), which has five levels from N5 (easiest) to N1. The JLPT does not publish official study-hour figures, but commonly cited estimates for learners with no Chinese-character background are roughly **300 to 500 hours to pass N5** and **600 to 900 hours to pass N4**. Treat these as ballparks; people who already read Chinese move much faster through kanji, and people who immerse (anime, games, a Japanese partner) pick up listening faster.

What that means in calendar time:

| Daily study | 16 weeks gives you | N4 exam-ready after |
|---|---|---|
| 30 minutes | kana, survival phrases, early N5 | 2 to 3 years |
| 1 hour | solid N5 basics, simple conversations | about 18 months |
| 2 hours (this plan) | all N5 grammar, most N4 grammar met once, ~250 kanji, slow real conversations | 6 to 9 months |
| 4+ hours (intensive) | N5 passed, strong N4 | 4 to 6 months |

The 16-week plan at the end of this guide assumes about two hours a day, roughly 220 hours. It gets you conversational in the everyday sense and walks you through almost every grammar point on the N4 syllabus. Passing N4 comfortably usually takes a few more months of reading, listening and review on top.

### How to study, in one list

1. **One spine, many supplements.** Pick one structured course and follow it in order: the free *Irodori* textbooks from the Japan Foundation, *Genki* (paid, the most common university textbook), or *Tae Kim's Guide* online. Everything else (apps, videos, podcasts) supports the spine. Switching courses every two weeks is the most common way people stall.
2. **Spaced repetition every single day.** Use Anki (or a similar SRS) for vocabulary and kanji. Fifteen to thirty minutes of reviews, never skipped. Missing days is what makes reviews pile up and people quit. Details in the study plan section.
3. **Listen more than you think you need to.** Comprehensible input (material you understand at 80 to 95 percent) is where grammar becomes instinct. Beginner podcasts and graded readers with audio are ideal.
4. **Speak from week one.** Read every example sentence aloud. *Shadow*: play a native line, then repeat it immediately with the same rhythm and melody. It is the single best pronunciation habit.
5. **Learn words in sentences.** 食べる (*taberu*, to eat) is easier to remember as パンを食べる (*pan o taberu*, eat bread), and you learn the particle at the same time.
6. **Accept being wrong.** Japanese speakers are famously kind to learners. A sentence with a wrong particle still gets you your coffee.

> **EXERCISE:** Write down how many minutes a day you can honestly commit for the next 16 weeks, and when (for example, 30 minutes on the train, 60 minutes after dinner, 30 minutes at lunch). Multiply by 112 days. Compare the total with the table above and adjust your expectations, not your honesty. *Answer:* there is no wrong answer, but if your total is below 100 hours, plan for the 16-week schedule to take 30 weeks instead.

## The three scripts

A normal Japanese sentence mixes three writing systems, and there are no spaces between words. That sounds chaotic until you notice that the scripts themselves do the job spaces do: the switch from kanji to hiragana usually marks where a word ends and its grammar begins.

![The sentence watashi wa koohii o nomimasu split by script, with panels describing hiragana, katakana and kanji](study/japanese-zero-to-conversational/three-scripts.svg "Kanji carry meaning, hiragana carry grammar, katakana mark foreign words")

- **Hiragana** (ひらがな) is a syllabary of 46 basic signs, each one a sound. It writes grammar: particles like は and を, verb and adjective endings (called *okurigana*: the みます in 飲みます), and native words that have no common kanji or whose kanji are rare. Children's books are written almost entirely in hiragana.
- **Katakana** (カタカナ) represents the same 46 sounds with angular shapes. It writes loanwords (コーヒー *koohii*, coffee; パン *pan*, bread, from Portuguese), foreign names (ジョン *Jon*), onomatopoeia, and emphasis, a bit like italics in English.
- **Kanji** (漢字) are characters borrowed from Chinese from around the 5th century onward. Each carries meaning and usually several readings. The official list of characters for general use (*joyo kanji*, revised in 2010) has 2,136 entries; for N4 you need about 300.

Why three? Chinese is a language with little inflection, so Chinese characters fit it well. Japanese piles endings onto its words (食べる, 食べない, 食べたい, 食べられなかった...), and characters cannot show those endings. Around the 9th century, scribes simplified kanji used purely for their sounds into two sets of phonetic signs: hiragana (from cursive forms of whole kanji) and katakana (from fragments of kanji). Today kanji hold the meaning-bearing stems, hiragana hold the grammar, and katakana hold the imports.

A few more facts you will meet early:

- **Furigana** are small kana printed above or beside a kanji to show its reading: 漢字 with かんじ on top. Learner materials and children's books use them heavily.
- Japanese can be written horizontally, left to right (*yokogaki*, used on the web and in most textbooks), or vertically, top to bottom and right to left (*tategaki*, used in novels, newspapers and manga).
- The sentence-ending full stop is 。 and the comma is 、.

Dakuten, handakuten and yoon work exactly as in hiragana: ガ *ga*, パ *pa*, キャ *kya*, ジュ *ju*, and so on. Katakana adds two things hiragana rarely needs:

- **The long-vowel bar ー**, which stretches the previous vowel: コーヒー *koohii* (coffee), ケーキ *keeki* (cake), ルール *ruuru* (rule).
- **Extended combinations for foreign sounds**, built with small vowels:

| Katakana | Romaji | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ファ フィ フェ フォ | fa fi fe fo | ファン *fan*, フォーク *fooku* | fan, fork |

| シェ ジェ チェ | she je che | シェフ *shefu*, チェック *chekku* | chef, check |
| トゥ ドゥ | tu du | タトゥー *tatuu* | tattoo |
| ヴ | vu | ヴァイオリン *vaiorin* (also バイオリン) | violin |

**Katakana look-alikes** are the classic trap. Stroke direction is the tell:

| Pair | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|
| シ *shi* vs ツ *tsu* | シ's dots sit on the left and its long stroke rises from bottom-left; ツ's dots sit on top and the stroke falls from top-right |
| ソ *so* vs ン *n* | ソ's short stroke is on top and the long one falls downward; ン's dot is at the left and the long stroke rises |
| ク *ku* vs ケ *ke* vs タ *ta* | ケ has a long horizontal bar and a separate falling stroke; タ is ク plus a small inner tick |
| ウ *u* vs ワ *wa* vs フ *fu* | ウ has a dot on top, ワ has a short left leg, フ has neither |

For hiragana, watch さ *sa* vs ち *chi* (mirror images), ぬ *nu* vs め *me* (ぬ has a loop), ね *ne* vs れ *re* vs わ *wa* (the ending stroke), and る *ru* vs ろ *ro* (る has a loop).

> **TIP:** Learn each kana with a picture mnemonic (Tofugu's free kana guides are excellent for this), then *write* each one five times while saying the sound aloud. Stroke order matters more than it seems: handwriting input on phones and dictionary lookup by drawing both depend on it, and kanji later reuse the same habits.

### Typing Japanese with an IME

You type Japanese with a romaji keyboard and an *Input Method Editor* (IME) that converts as you go. You type `taberu`, the IME shows たべる, you press Space to cycle through kanji candidates (食べる), and Enter to confirm.

- **Windows 11:** Settings > Time & language > Language & region > Add a language > Japanese. The Microsoft IME is included. Switch keyboards with Win + Space, and toggle between direct English input and kana input with Alt + ` (the key left of 1) or the 半角/全角 key on Japanese keyboards.
- **macOS:** System Settings > Keyboard > Text Input > Edit > add Japanese - Romaji. Switch with Ctrl + Space (or the Globe key).
- **Phones:** add the Japanese keyboard in iOS or Gboard on Android. Most Japanese people use the 12-key "flick" layout, but the QWERTY romaji layout is fine for learners.
- **Google Japanese Input** is a free alternative IME on Windows and macOS.

The keystrokes that confuse beginners:

| To get | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ん | `nn` | `konnnichiha` (three n) gives こんにちは; a single `n` before a vowel becomes な, に, ぬ... |
| っ (small tsu) | double the consonant | `kitte` gives きって |
| を | `wo` | even though it is pronounced *o* |
| は (particle) | `ha` | the IME types what is written, not what is said |
| づ / ぢ | `du` / `di` | |
| ゃ, ぁ, っ alone | `xya`, `xa`, `xtu` (or `l` instead of `x`) | small kana |
| ー | `-` | long-vowel bar |
| katakana | type, then F7 | F6 converts to hiragana, F10 to romaji |

## Pronunciation

### Five vowels and the mora beat

Japanese has five vowels, all short and pure: *a* as in "father", *i* as in "machine", *u* as in "food" but with unrounded lips, *e* as in "bet", *o* as in "pole" without the glide. No diphthongs slide around the way English "go" or "day" do.

The rhythm unit is the **mora**: every kana (including ん, small っ, and the long-vowel extension) takes one equal beat. English speakers naturally squash words into syllables and stress one of them; Japanese gives each beat roughly the same length.

| Word | Beats | Count |
|---|---|---|
| ねこ *neko* (cat) | ね・こ | 2 |
| とうきょう *toukyou* (Tokyo) | と・う・きょ・う | 4 (not "To-kyo", 2) |
| にっぽん *nippon* (Japan) | に・っ・ぽ・ん | 4 |
| がっこう *gakkou* (school) | が・っ・こ・う | 4 |
| コーヒー *koohii* (coffee) | コ・ー・ヒ・ー | 4 |

### Long vowels and the small tsu change meaning

Length is not decoration. Holding a vowel for two beats, or pausing for one beat before a consonant, produces a different word.

| Short | Long / doubled | |
|---|---|---|
| おばさん *obasan*, aunt | おばあさん *obaasan*, grandmother | vowel length |
| おじさん *ojisan*, uncle | おじいさん *ojiisan*, grandfather | vowel length |
| ビル *biru*, building | ビール *biiru*, beer | vowel length |
| ここ *koko*, here | こうこう *koukou*, high school | vowel length |
| きて *kite*, come (te-form) | きって *kitte*, postage stamp | small っ |
| さか *saka*, slope | さっか *sakka*, author | small っ |

Spelling rules for long vowels in hiragana: *aa* is written ああ, *ii* いい, *uu* うう, *ee* is usually えい (せんせい *sensei*, pronounced close to "sen-see"), and *oo* is usually おう (ありがとう *arigatou*, with a long o). Katakana uses ー for all of them.

The small っ is a one-beat silent stop: in きって, your tongue is ready for the *t* and holds for a beat before releasing it.

### Consonants that need attention

- **R** is a single tap of the tongue behind the teeth, between English r, l and d. Say "ladder" quickly in American English: the dd is close to Japanese ら.
- **ふ (fu)** is made with both lips, like blowing out a candle, not with teeth on the lip.
- **ん** takes a full beat. It sounds like *m* before b, m and p (さんぽ *sanpo*, a walk, sounds "sampo") and like *ng* before k and g (ぎんこう *ginkou*, bank).
- **Vowel devoicing:** *i* and *u* between voiceless consonants, or at the end after one, are often whispered. です sounds like "des", します like "shimas", すき (like) like "ski".
- **が** in the middle of a word is sometimes nasalized (*nga*) by older and broadcast speakers. You do not need to produce it.

### Pitch accent, briefly

Japanese does not stress syllables by loudness; each mora is high or low in pitch, and the pattern belongs to the word. In standard (Tokyo) Japanese:

| Word | Pitch | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| はし (HL) | *HA*-shi | chopsticks (箸) |
| はし (LH, drops after) | ha-*SHI* (が falls) | bridge (橋) |
| はし (LH, stays up) | ha-*SHI* (が stays high) | edge (端) |
| あめ (HL) | *A*-me | rain (雨) |
| あめ (LH) | a-*ME* | candy (飴) |

Context almost always disambiguates, and native speakers from Osaka and Tokyo use different patterns without confusing each other. Wrong pitch is mostly an accent issue, not a comprehension issue. My advice: do not memorize pitch tables at this stage, but shadow audio from day one so your melody is copied from real speakers. If you are curious, the free OJAD (Online Japanese Accent Dictionary) from the University of Tokyo shows pitch curves for words and whole sentences.

> **EXERCISE:** Count the morae: (1) きょう *kyou*, today (2) きよう *kiyou*, skillful (3) しんぶん *shinbun*, newspaper (4) ちょっと *chotto* (5) ラーメン *raamen*. Then say each while tapping the table once per beat. *Answers:* (1) 2: きょ・う (2) 3: き・よ・う (3) 4: し・ん・ぶ・ん (4) 3: ちょ・っ・と (5) 4: ラ・ー・メ・ン.

## Kanji strategy

### What a kanji is made of

Most kanji are built from smaller parts. One part, the **radical**, often hints at the meaning category; another often hints at the reading. Dictionaries traditionally sort kanji under 214 radicals. You do not need all of them, but recognizing thirty or so common components turns kanji from random drawings into Lego.

| Component | Meaning | Appears in |
|---|---|---|
| 亻 (人) | person | 休 rest (a person leaning on a tree 木), 体 body, 何 what |
| 氵 (水) | water | 海 sea, 泳 swim, 酒 alcohol, 洗 wash |
| 言 / 訁 | words, speech | 話 talk, 語 language, 読 read, 計 measure |
| 木 | tree, wood | 林 grove (two trees), 森 forest (three), 本 book/origin |

| 辶 | movement, road | 近 near, 道 road, 週 week |

### On and kun readings

Most kanji have two families of readings:

- **Kun'yomi** (訓読み) is the native Japanese word attached to the character. It is used when the kanji stands alone or carries hiragana endings: 山 *yama* (mountain), 食べる *taberu* (eat).
- **On'yomi** (音読み) is the Japanese approximation of the Chinese pronunciation. It is used mostly in compounds of two or more kanji: 富士山 *Fujisan* (Mount Fuji), 食堂 *shokudou* (cafeteria).

Dictionaries write kun readings in hiragana and on readings in katakana. The rule of thumb (alone or with okurigana: kun; in compounds: on) holds most of the time. Exceptions exist, including whole-word readings like 今日 *kyou* (today), 明日 *ashita* (tomorrow), 大人 *otona* (adult) and 一人 *hitori* (one person), which you just learn as words.

### How to learn about 300 kanji without burning out

- **Learn kanji through words you already need.** When you learn 学生 (student), you learn 学 and 生. Pure character drilling without vocabulary is a trap at this level.
- **Recognition first, handwriting second.** You will read far more than you write by hand. Write each new kanji a few times to fix the shape and stroke order, then review by reading.
- **Five to ten new kanji a day** is plenty. At seven a day, you pass 300 in about six weeks of pure kanji time; spread across 16 weeks alongside grammar, 250 is realistic.
- **Use an SRS deck with vocabulary cards** (front: 学生, back: がくせい, student, plus audio). WaniKani's first three levels are free and teach radicals-to-kanji-to-vocabulary in order; many learners use them as a trial of that method.
- **Use furigana as training wheels, then remove them.** NHK News Web Easy lets you toggle furigana on and off.

## Sentence structure and particles

### The skeleton: verb last, particles after

English relies on word order: "the dog bites the man" and "the man bites the dog" differ only in order. Japanese relies on **particles**, short words placed *after* a noun to mark its role. The one fixed rule is that the verb (or adjective, or です) comes at the end. The rest can shuffle, with the most important new information usually placed closest to the verb.

![A sentence divided into chunks, each tagged by its particle, with the verb at the end, plus two reorderings and an SOV versus SVO comparison](study/japanese-zero-to-conversational/sentence-skeleton.svg "Particles tag roles, so chunks can move; only the verb is pinned to the end")

### The simplest sentence: A は B です

The workhorse pattern is *A wa B desu*, "A is B". です (*desu*) is the polite copula (the verb "to be" for nouns).

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 私は学生です。 | Watashi wa gakusei desu. | I am a student. |
| 私は学生じゃありません。 | Watashi wa gakusei ja arimasen. | I am not a student. |
| 田中さんは先生でした。 | Tanaka-san wa sensei deshita. | Ms./Mr. Tanaka was a teacher. |
| 田中さんは先生ですか。 | Tanaka-san wa sensei desu ka. | Is Tanaka-san a teacher? |

Four facts from those four lines:

- Negative です is じゃありません (*ja arimasen*); in writing and formal speech ではありません (*dewa arimasen*), and in relaxed polite speech じゃないです (*ja nai desu*).
- Past is でした (*deshita*); past negative is じゃありませんでした (*ja arimasen deshita*).
- A question is just the statement plus か (*ka*), with no word-order change. In writing, the か replaces the question mark.
- Once the topic is known, drop it. If someone asks 学生ですか, you answer はい、学生です (*hai, gakusei desu*), not "yes, I am a student" with 私は.

### The core particles

| Particle | Romaji | Main job | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| は | wa | topic: "as for X" | 私は学生です。 *Watashi wa gakusei desu.* | I'm a student. |
| が | ga | subject; new or identified information | 猫がいます。 *Neko ga imasu.* | There's a cat. |
| を | o | direct object | 水を飲みます。 *Mizu o nomimasu.* | I drink water. |
| に | ni | point in time; destination; location of existence; recipient | 七時に起きます。 *Shichiji ni okimasu.* | I get up at seven. |
| で | de | place where an action happens; means, tool | 図書館で勉強します。 *Toshokan de benkyou shimasu.* | I study at the library. |
| へ | e | direction, toward | 日本へ行きます。 *Nihon e ikimasu.* | I'm going to Japan. |
| と | to | complete "and" between nouns; "with" a person | 友達と話します。 *Tomodachi to hanashimasu.* | I talk with a friend. |
| も | mo | also, too (replaces は, が, を) | 私も学生です。 *Watashi mo gakusei desu.* | I'm a student too. |
| の | no | possession, linking nouns | 私の本 *watashi no hon*; 日本語の先生 *nihongo no sensei* | my book; Japanese teacher |
| か | ka | question (sentence end) | 何ですか。 *Nan desu ka.* | What is it? |
| よ | yo | "I'm telling you", new info to the listener | おいしいですよ。 *Oishii desu yo.* | It's tasty, you know. |
| ね | ne | "right?", seeking agreement | 暑いですね。 *Atsui desu ne.* | Hot, isn't it? |

Some points that the table cannot hold:

- **に vs で for places.** に marks where something *is* or where you *go*; で marks where something *happens*. 公園にいます (*kouen ni imasu*): I'm at the park. 公園で遊びます (*kouen de asobimasu*): I play at the park.
- **に with time.** Clock times, dates and days take に: 月曜日に (*getsuyoubi ni*, on Monday). Relative time words do not: 今日, 明日, 毎日, 来週 (*kyou, ashita, mainichi, raishuu*) stand alone.
- **へ vs に for destinations.** Both work with 行く (to go); へ emphasizes the direction, に the arrival point. に is more common in speech.
- **と vs や.** と lists everything: パンと卵 (*pan to tamago*) is "bread and eggs, that's all". や (*ya*) gives examples: パンや卵 is "bread, eggs, and so on".
- **Particles stack.** 友達とも (*tomodachi to mo*, with friends too), 日本では (*Nihon de wa*, as for in Japan), 私にも (*watashi ni mo*, to me as well). は and も replace が and を, but sit after に, で, と.

### は versus が: the intuition

This is the question every learner asks, and no one-line rule covers it. Here is a set of intuitions that will carry you to N4 and beyond.

1. **は sets the frame; が picks the player.** は says "as for X, here's the comment". が points at *which one* does or is something. 象は鼻が長い (*zou wa hana ga nagai*): "As for elephants, the nose is long." The whole sentence is about elephants; within that frame, the nose is the thing that is long.
2. **New information takes が, known information takes は.** A story starts with が and continues with は: 昔々、おじいさんがいました。おじいさんは山へ行きました。 (*Mukashi mukashi, ojiisan ga imashita. Ojiisan wa yama e ikimashita.*) "Once upon a time there was an old man. The old man went to the mountains."
3. **Question words always take が, and so do the answers.** 誰が来ましたか。田中さんが来ました。 (*Dare ga kimashita ka. Tanaka-san ga kimashita.*) "Who came? Tanaka came." You cannot say 誰は.
4. **が can mean "it is X (and not someone else)".** 私は田中です: "I'm Tanaka" (introducing yourself). 私が田中です: "*I'm* Tanaka" (the Tanaka you're looking for).
5. **は contrasts.** 肉は食べますが、魚は食べません。 (*Niku wa tabemasu ga, sakana wa tabemasen.*) "Meat I eat, but fish I don't." In negative sentences, は often replaces を or が for this reason: お酒は飲みません (*osake wa nomimasen*, I don't drink alcohol).
6. **Some predicates take their "object" with が.** Likes, dislikes, skill, understanding, wanting and ability: 私は寿司が好きです (*watashi wa sushi ga suki desu*, I like sushi), 日本語が分かります (*nihongo ga wakarimasu*, I understand Japanese), 車がほしいです (*kuruma ga hoshii desu*, I want a car). Also ある and いる: 時間があります (*jikan ga arimasu*, I have time).
7. **Inside a clause that modifies a noun, the subject takes が.** 私が作ったケーキ (*watashi ga tsukutta keeki*): "the cake that I made". Never は there.

## Verbs: groups and conjugation

### Polite forms first

Japanese verbs do not change for person or number: 食べます is "I eat", "you eat", "they eat". They have only two tenses: **non-past** (present habits and the future) and **past**. The polite *masu* form is what you use with anyone you do not know well, so learn it first.

| | Non-past | Negative | Past | Past negative | "Let's" |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| eat | 食べます *tabemasu* | 食べません *tabemasen* | 食べました *tabemashita* | 食べませんでした *tabemasen deshita* | 食べましょう *tabemashou* |
| write | 書きます *kakimasu* | 書きません *kakimasen* | 書きました *kakimashita* | 書きませんでした *kakimasen deshita* | 書きましょう *kakimashou* |
| do | します *shimasu* | しません *shimasen* | しました *shimashita* | しませんでした *shimasen deshita* | しましょう *shimashou* |
| come | 来ます *kimasu* | 来ません *kimasen* | 来ました *kimashita* | 来ませんでした *kimasen deshita* | 来ましょう *kimashou* |

明日東京に行きます (*ashita Toukyou ni ikimasu*) is "I'll go to Tokyo tomorrow"; 毎日行きます (*mainichi ikimasu*) is "I go every day". Context and time words carry the difference. To invite someone, use the negative question: 一緒に行きませんか (*issho ni ikimasen ka*), "Won't you go with me?", which is softer than 行きましょう, "Let's go".

### The three verb groups

To build any form other than *masu*, you need the **dictionary form** (the plain non-past, the form listed in dictionaries), and you need to know which of three groups the verb belongs to.

![Flowchart classifying a verb as irregular, u-verb or ru-verb, with the exception list and masu examples](study/japanese-zero-to-conversational/verb-groups.svg "Three questions sort every verb; memorize the handful of exceptions")

| Group | Also called | How to spot it | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | u-verbs, godan (five-row) | anything not in group 2 or 3; the final -u changes | 書く *kaku*, 飲む *nomu*, 話す *hanasu*, 買う *kau*, 待つ *matsu*, 帰る *kaeru* |
| 2 | ru-verbs, ichidan (one-row) | ends in -iru or -eru; drop る to get the stem | 食べる *taberu*, 見る *miru*, 起きる *okiru*, 寝る *neru* |
| 3 | irregular | just two, plus compounds | する *suru* (do), 来る *kuru* (come); 勉強する *benkyou suru* (study) |

The trap is that some -iru and -eru verbs are really group 1. The common ones at this level: 帰る *kaeru* (return home), 入る *hairu* (enter), 走る *hashiru* (run), 知る *shiru* (know), 切る *kiru* (cut), 要る *iru* (need), 喋る *shaberu* (chat). Any verb ending in -aru, -oru or -uru (ある, 取る, 作る) is group 1.

### Why group 1 is called "five-row"

Group 1 verbs change their last kana along the vowel row of the gojuon chart, and each vowel feeds a different form. Take 書く (*kaku*): its last kana く sits in the k-row か き く け こ.

| Vowel | Kana | Form it builds | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | か | negative (+ ない) | 書かない *kakanai* |
| i | き | masu stem (+ ます) | 書きます *kakimasu* |
| u | く | dictionary form | 書く *kaku* |
| e | け | potential (+ る), command | 書ける *kakeru* |
| o | こ | volitional (+ う) | 書こう *kakou* |

One wrinkle: verbs ending in う use わ, not あ, for the negative. 買う → 買わない (*kawanai*), 会う → 会わない (*awanai*).

Group 2 is simpler: drop る and attach the ending directly. 食べる → 食べない, 食べます, 食べられる, 食べよう.

### The plain forms

Plain forms are what friends use with each other, and they are also the building blocks of most grammar patterns (〜と思います, 〜ことがあります, noun-modifying clauses). This is the table to know cold.

| Dictionary | Group | Negative (nai) | Past (ta) | Past negative | Te-form |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 食べる *taberu* eat | 2 | 食べない *tabenai* | 食べた *tabeta* | 食べなかった *tabenakatta* | 食べて *tabete* |
| 見る *miru* see | 2 | 見ない *minai* | 見た *mita* | 見なかった *minakatta* | 見て *mite* |
| 書く *kaku* write | 1 | 書かない *kakanai* | 書いた *kaita* | 書かなかった *kakanakatta* | 書いて *kaite* |
| 泳ぐ *oyogu* swim | 1 | 泳がない *oyoganai* | 泳いだ *oyoida* | 泳がなかった *oyoganakatta* | 泳いで *oyoide* |
| 話す *hanasu* speak | 1 | 話さない *hanasanai* | 話した *hanashita* | 話さなかった *hanasanakatta* | 話して *hanashite* |
| 待つ *matsu* wait | 1 | 待たない *matanai* | 待った *matta* | 待たなかった *matanakatta* | 待って *matte* |
| 飲む *nomu* drink | 1 | 飲まない *nomanai* | 飲んだ *nonda* | 飲まなかった *nomanakatta* | 飲んで *nonde* |
| 遊ぶ *asobu* play | 1 | 遊ばない *asobanai* | 遊んだ *asonda* | 遊ばなかった *asobanakatta* | 遊んで *asonde* |

| する *suru* do | 3 | しない *shinai* | した *shita* | しなかった *shinakatta* | して *shite* |

Note that 来る changes its vowel: *ku*ru, *ko*nai, *ki*ta, *ki*te, *ki*masu. The kanji stays the same, so watch the reading.

The negative ない form conjugates like an i-adjective (covered below): 食べない → 食べなかった.

### The te-form

The te-form is the most useful form in the language. It links actions ("I got up *and* ate breakfast"), makes requests (〜てください), and builds a whole family of patterns (〜ている, 〜てもいい, 〜てはいけない). For group 2 it is trivial (食べる → 食べて). For group 1, the ending depends on the last kana of the dictionary form.

![Map from dictionary-form endings to te-form endings with example verbs for each group](study/japanese-zero-to-conversational/te-form-map.svg "The te-form rules: memorize the five sound changes and the single exception, 行く")

| Dictionary ending | Te-form | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| う, つ, る | って | 買う → 買って, 待つ → 待って, 帰る → 帰って |
| む, ぶ, ぬ | んで | 飲む → 飲んで, 遊ぶ → 遊んで, 死ぬ → 死んで |
| く | いて | 書く → 書いて, 聞く → 聞いて |
| ぐ | いで | 泳ぐ → 泳いで, 急ぐ → 急いで |
| す | して | 話す → 話して, 貸す → 貸して |
| 行く (exception) | 行って | not 行いて |
| group 2: る | て | 食べる → 食べて, 見る → 見て |
| する, 来る | して, 来て | 勉強して, 持って来て |

Many Japanese classes teach these rules as a chant sung to the tune of "Clementine" (search for "te-form song" and you will find dozens of versions). It sounds silly and it works: you will be humming it in exams for years. If singing is not your thing, chant the pairs in rhythm: *u-tsu-ru: tte; mu-bu-nu: nde; ku: ite; gu: ide; su: shite; iku is itte*.

The **plain past (ta-form)** follows exactly the same sound changes with た and だ: 書いた, 飲んだ, 行った, 話した. Learn one and you have the other.

Linking actions with the te-form puts them in sequence, and only the last verb carries the tense and politeness:

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 朝ご飯を食べて、学校に行きました。 | Asagohan o tabete, gakkou ni ikimashita. | I ate breakfast and went to school. |
| 家に帰って、シャワーを浴びて、寝ます。 | Ie ni kaette, shawaa o abite, nemasu. | I'll go home, shower and go to bed. |

### Potential: "can do"

The potential form says what you are able to do. The object usually switches from を to が.

| Group | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | final -u → -e + る | 話す → 話せる *hanaseru*; 飲む → 飲める *nomeru*; 行く → 行ける *ikeru* |
| 2 | る → られる | 食べる → 食べられる *taberareru*; 見る → 見られる *mirareru* |
| 3 | irregular | する → できる *dekiru*; 来る → 来られる *korareru* |

日本語が少し話せます (*nihongo ga sukoshi hanasemasu*): "I can speak a little Japanese." 納豆が食べられません (*nattou ga taberaremasen*): "I can't eat natto." For activities expressed with a noun, use ができる: テニスができます (*tenisu ga dekimasu*), "I can play tennis".

Potential forms are themselves group 2 verbs, so they conjugate like 食べる: 話せない, 話せた.

> **NOTE:** In casual speech you will constantly hear *ra-nuki* ("ra-dropped") potentials: 見れる instead of 見られる, 食べれる instead of 食べられる. They are normal in conversation among younger speakers, but use the full form in writing, in exams, and with anyone you address politely.

### Volitional: "let's" and "I'll"

The plain volitional is the casual version of 〜ましょう.

| Group | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | final -u → -o + う | 行く → 行こう *ikou*; 飲む → 飲もう *nomou*; 帰る → 帰ろう *kaerou* |
| 2 | る → よう | 食べる → 食べよう *tabeyou*; 見る → 見よう *miyou* |
| 3 | irregular | する → しよう *shiyou*; 来る → 来よう *koyou* |

Uses: 一緒に食べよう (*issho ni tabeyou*, let's eat together); 手伝いましょうか (*tetsudaimashou ka*, shall I help?); and with 〜と思う for intentions, 来年日本に行こうと思います (*rainen Nihon ni ikou to omoimasu*, I'm thinking of going to Japan next year).

## Adjectives and existence

### Two kinds of adjectives

Japanese adjectives come in two families. **I-adjectives** end in い and conjugate themselves, like verbs. **Na-adjectives** behave like nouns: they need な before a noun and use です/だ for tense.

| Form | i-adjective 高い *takai* (expensive, tall) | na-adjective 静か *shizuka* (quiet) |
|---|---|---|
| before a noun | 高い本 *takai hon* | 静か**な**部屋 *shizuka na heya* |
| polite non-past | 高いです | 静かです |
| polite negative | 高くないです / 高くありません | 静かじゃないです / 静かではありません |
| polite past | 高かったです | 静かでした |
| polite past negative | 高くなかったです | 静かじゃなかったです |
| plain non-past | 高い | 静かだ |
| plain negative | 高くない | 静かじゃない |
| plain past | 高かった | 静かだった |
| te-form (linking) | 高くて | 静かで |
| adverb | 高く | 静かに |

- The irregular one: いい (*ii*, good) conjugates from its older form よい: よくない, よかった, よくて. かっこいい (cool) does the same: かっこよかった.
- Linking adjectives uses the te-form: 安くておいしい (*yasukute oishii*, cheap and tasty), 静かできれいな町 (*shizuka de kirei na machi*, a quiet, pretty town).
- **Traps:** きれい (*kirei*, pretty, clean), 有名 (*yuumei*, famous), 嫌い (*kirai*, disliked) and 好き (*suki*, liked) are na-adjectives even though some end in い. きれいな花 (a pretty flower), never きれい花 or きれくない.

| 大きい ookii | big | 好き suki | liked |
| 小さい chiisai | small | 嫌い kirai | disliked |
| 新しい atarashii | new | 上手 jouzu | skillful |
| 古い furui | old (things) | 下手 heta | unskillful |
| 安い yasui | cheap | 元気 genki | healthy, energetic |
| おいしい oishii | tasty | 便利 benri | convenient |
| 楽しい tanoshii | fun | 大切 taisetsu | important, precious |
| 難しい muzukashii | difficult | 簡単 kantan | easy, simple |
| 忙しい isogashii | busy | 暇 hima | free (time) |
| 近い chikai | near | 大丈夫 daijoubu | OK, fine |

### There is: ある and いる

Japanese has two verbs of existence, and the choice depends on whether the thing can move by itself.

| Verb | Used for | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| ある *aru* (あります) | inanimate things, plants, events | 机の上に本があります。 *Tsukue no ue ni hon ga arimasu.* | There's a book on the desk. |
| いる *iru* (います) | people, animals | 公園に子供がいます。 *Kouen ni kodomo ga imasu.* | There are children in the park. |

Two word orders, two meanings:

- **Place に thing が あります**: introduces something new. 駅の前にコンビニがあります: "There's a convenience store in front of the station."
- **Thing は place に あります**: tells where a known thing is. コンビニは駅の前にあります: "The convenience store is in front of the station." Shorter in speech: コンビニは駅の前です.

ある and いる also express having: 兄が二人います (*ani ga futari imasu*, I have two older brothers), 時間がありません (*jikan ga arimasen*, I don't have time). The plain negative of ある is simply ない; of いる, いない.

Position words are nouns, linked with の:

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 上 / 下 | ue / shita | on, above / under, below |
| 中 / 外 | naka / soto | inside / outside |
| 前 / 後ろ | mae / ushiro | in front / behind |
| 隣 / 横 | tonari / yoko | next to (same kind) / beside |
| 右 / 左 | migi / hidari | right / left |
| 近く / 間 | chikaku / aida | near / between |

## Everyday grammar patterns

These are the patterns that turn textbook sentences into things you actually say. Most attach to a verb form you already know.

| Pattern | Attaches to | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 〜たい | masu stem | want to | 日本に行きたいです。 *Nihon ni ikitai desu.* I want to go to Japan. |
| 〜てください | te-form | please do | ちょっと待ってください。 *Chotto matte kudasai.* Please wait a moment. |
| 〜ないでください | nai-form | please don't | ここで写真を撮らないでください。 *Koko de shashin o toranaide kudasai.* Please don't take photos here. |
| 〜ている | te-form | ongoing action; resulting state; habit | 今、雨が降っています。 *Ima, ame ga futte imasu.* It's raining now. |
| 〜てもいい | te-form | may, it's OK to | 窓を開けてもいいですか。 *Mado o akete mo ii desu ka.* May I open the window? |
| 〜てはいけない | te-form | must not | ここでたばこを吸ってはいけません。 *Koko de tabako o sutte wa ikemasen.* You must not smoke here. |
| 〜なければならない | nai-form minus い | must, have to | 明日早く起きなければなりません。 *Ashita hayaku okinakereba narimasen.* I have to get up early tomorrow. |
| 〜たことがある | ta-form | have done (experience) | 富士山に登ったことがあります。 *Fujisan ni nobotta koto ga arimasu.* I have climbed Mt. Fuji. |
| 〜と思う | plain form | I think | 明日は雨だと思います。 *Ashita wa ame da to omoimasu.* I think it'll rain tomorrow. |
| 〜から | any form | because, so | 暑いから、窓を開けます。 *Atsui kara, mado o akemasu.* It's hot, so I'll open the window. |
| 〜が / 〜けど | any form | but | 高いですが、おいしいです。 *Takai desu ga, oishii desu.* It's expensive, but tasty. |
| 〜ほうがいい | ta-form / nai-form | you'd better | 早く寝たほうがいいですよ。 *Hayaku neta hou ga ii desu yo.* You'd better go to bed early. |
| 〜たら | ta-form + ら | if, when (once) | 駅に着いたら、電話してください。 *Eki ni tsuitara, denwa shite kudasai.* Call me when you reach the station. |
| 〜前に / 〜後で | dictionary / ta-form | before / after | 寝る前に歯を磨きます。 *Neru mae ni ha o migakimasu.* I brush my teeth before bed. |
| 〜に行く | masu stem | go (in order) to | 映画を見に行きます。 *Eiga o mi ni ikimasu.* I'm going to see a movie. |

### Notes on the big ones

**〜たい** turns a verb into an i-adjective: 行きたい, 行きたくない (don't want to go), 行きたかった (wanted to go). The object may take が or を: 水が飲みたい and 水を飲みたい are both fine. It describes *your* wishes; for other people, Japanese avoids stating their feelings directly and says 行きたがっています (*ikitagatte imasu*, seems to want to go) or asks 行きたいですか.

**〜ている** has three main readings, and the verb decides which:

- Action verbs: ongoing action. 本を読んでいます (*hon o yonde imasu*), I'm reading a book.
- Change-of-state verbs: the resulting state. 結婚しています (*kekkon shite imasu*), I'm married (not "I'm getting married"). 窓が開いています (*mado ga aite imasu*), the window is open.
- Habits and jobs: 毎朝走っています (*maiasa hashitte imasu*), I run every morning; 銀行で働いています (*ginkou de hataraite imasu*), I work at a bank.

The famous pair: 知っています (*shitte imasu*), "I know", but the negative is 知りません (*shirimasen*), "I don't know". Not 知っていません. In casual speech 〜ている shrinks to 〜てる: 何してる？ (*nani shiteru?*, what are you doing?).

**〜なければならない** is long, so speech shortens it constantly: 〜なければなりません (formal), 〜ないといけません (common spoken), 〜なきゃ (casual). 行かなきゃ！ (*ikanakya!*): "I've got to go!" Its opposite, "don't have to", is 〜なくてもいい: 行かなくてもいいです (*ikanakute mo ii desu*), "you don't have to go".

**Giving and receiving** is the other N4 topic that trips people up, because the verb depends on the direction relative to you:

| Verb | Direction | Example |
|---|---|---|
| あげる *ageru* | I (or anyone) give to someone else | 友達に本をあげました。 I gave my friend a book. |
| くれる *kureru* | someone gives to me or my group | 友達が本をくれました。 My friend gave me a book. |
| もらう *morau* | I receive from someone | 友達に本をもらいました。 I got a book from my friend. |

With the te-form these describe favors: 教えてくれました (*oshiete kuremashita*), "they kindly taught me". Adding くれる is how Japanese expresses gratitude for an action inside the grammar.

**Modifying nouns with whole clauses** works by putting a plain-form clause directly before the noun, with no "who" or "that": 昨日見た映画 (*kinou mita eiga*), "the movie I saw yesterday"; 眼鏡をかけている人 (*megane o kakete iru hito*), "the person wearing glasses".

## Casual, polite and keigo

Japanese builds social distance into its verb endings. You are always choosing a register, and the choice says something about your relationship with the listener.

![A staircase rising from casual through polite to the two branches of keigo, with eat and go at each level](study/japanese-zero-to-conversational/politeness-ladder.svg "Three registers: learners master polite, pick up casual with friends, and learn to recognize keigo")

### Plain vs polite, side by side

| Polite | Casual | English |
|---|---|---|
| 行きます | 行く | I go |
| 行きません | 行かない | I don't go |
| 行きました | 行った | I went |
| 行きませんでした | 行かなかった | I didn't go |
| 行きましょう | 行こう | Let's go |
| 学生です | 学生だ (often just 学生) | I'm a student |
| 高いです | 高い (no だ after i-adjectives) | It's expensive |
| 静かです | 静かだ | It's quiet |
| 行きますか | 行く？ (rising intonation) | Are you going? |
| 何ですか | 何？ / 何なの？ | What is it? |

Casual speech has its own habits: particles like は, を and が get dropped (ご飯食べた？ *gohan tabeta?*, did you eat?), questions often end in の (どこ行くの？ *doko iku no?*), and forms contract: 〜ている → 〜てる, 〜ては → 〜ちゃ, 〜なければ → 〜なきゃ, じゃない for ではない.

Who gets which:

- **Casual (tameguchi):** close friends, family, children, and people clearly younger than you in a relaxed setting.
- **Polite:** everyone else, including teachers, coworkers, shop staff and anyone you just met. This is your default, and it is never wrong for a learner. When a Japanese acquaintance switches to casual with you and says タメ口でいいよ (*tameguchi de ii yo*, "you can talk casually"), follow their lead.
- **Keigo (honorific language):** customers, bosses and clients in business, and formal situations. You will *hear* it constantly (every shop, train announcement and website uses it), so recognizing it early pays off even if you rarely produce it.

The social idea underneath is *uchi* and *soto*: your in-group (family, company, team) versus outsiders. You raise outsiders' actions and lower your own group's, which is why you call your own mother 母 when talking to a stranger (see the family terms below).

### Keigo in two directions

**Sonkeigo** (respectful) raises the other person's actions. **Kenjougo** (humble) lowers your own. A handful of verbs have special forms:

| Plain | Sonkeigo (their action) | Kenjougo (my action) | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| いる | いらっしゃる | おる | be, stay |
| 行く / 来る | いらっしゃる | 参る *mairu* | go / come |
| 食べる / 飲む | 召し上がる *meshiagaru* | いただく | eat / drink |
| 言う | おっしゃる | 申す *mousu* | say |
| する | なさる | いたす | do |
| 見る | ご覧になる *goran ni naru* | 拝見する *haiken suru* | see, look |
| 知っている | ご存じだ *gozonji da* | 存じている *zonjite iru* | know |
| くれる / もらう | くださる | いただく | give me / receive |

For other verbs there are two templates: sonkeigo お + masu stem + になる (お待ちになる, *omachi ni naru*, [you] wait) and kenjougo お + masu stem + する (お待ちします, *omachi shimasu*, I'll wait for you).

Never use any of these on your own name. Japanese names are said family name first: 山田花子 is *Yamada Hanako*, and you call her 山田さん until invited to use her given name.

## Vocabulary by theme

### Family

![Table of family words: plain terms for your own family and honorific terms for someone else's](study/japanese-zero-to-conversational/family-terms.svg "Two sets of family words: humble for your own family, honorific for others' and for addressing your elders")

## Dialogues

Read each dialogue aloud three times: once slowly with the romaji, once from the Japanese column only, and once shadowing at natural speed (record yourself if you can). Then cover the Japanese and try to produce it from the English.

### 1. Meeting a classmate (polite)

| Speaker | Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mike | はじめまして。マイクです。 | Hajimemashite. Maiku desu. | Nice to meet you. I'm Mike. |
| Yui | はじめまして。佐藤ゆいです。マイクさんはどこから来ましたか。 | Hajimemashite. Satou Yui desu. Maiku-san wa doko kara kimashita ka. | Nice to meet you. I'm Yui Sato. Where are you from, Mike? |
| Mike | カナダから来ました。留学生です。 | Kanada kara kimashita. Ryuugakusei desu. | I'm from Canada. I'm an exchange student. |
| Yui | そうですか。日本語が上手ですね。 | Sou desu ka. Nihongo ga jouzu desu ne. | Oh, really? Your Japanese is good. |
| Mike | いいえ、まだまだです。佐藤さんの趣味は何ですか。 | Iie, madamada desu. Satou-san no shumi wa nan desu ka. | No, I still have a long way to go. What are your hobbies? |
| Yui | テニスとアニメです。週末はよくテニスをします。 | Tenisu to anime desu. Shuumatsu wa yoku tenisu o shimasu. | Tennis and anime. I often play tennis on weekends. |
| Mike | いいですね。私もテニスが好きです。 | Ii desu ne. Watashi mo tenisu ga suki desu. | Nice. I like tennis too. |
| Yui | じゃあ、今度一緒にしませんか。 | Jaa, kondo issho ni shimasen ka. | Then why don't we play together sometime? |
| Mike | ぜひ！よろしくお願いします。 | Zehi! Yoroshiku onegaishimasu. | I'd love to! |

Notes: 日本語が上手ですね is a compliment you will hear after saying almost anything; the expected answer is modest (いいえ、まだまだです). 〜ませんか is the standard polite invitation.

### 2. Ordering at a cafe

| Speaker | Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Staff | いらっしゃいませ。ご注文はお決まりですか。 | Irasshaimase. Go-chuumon wa o-kimari desu ka. | Welcome. Have you decided on your order? |
| You | ホットコーヒーを一つと、チーズケーキを一つお願いします。 | Hotto koohii o hitotsu to, chiizu keeki o hitotsu onegaishimasu. | One hot coffee and one cheesecake, please. |
| Staff | サイズはいかがなさいますか。 | Saizu wa ikaga nasaimasu ka. | What size would you like? |
| You | Mでお願いします。 | Emu de onegaishimasu. | Medium, please. |
| Staff | 店内でお召し上がりですか。 | Tennai de o-meshiagari desu ka. | Will you be eating in? |
| You | はい、店内で。 | Hai, tennai de. | Yes, for here. |
| Staff | かしこまりました。合計で九百八十円です。 | Kashikomarimashita. Goukei de kyuuhyaku hachijuu en desu. | Certainly. That's 980 yen in total. |
| You | カードで払えますか。 | Kaado de haraemasu ka. | Can I pay by card? |
| Staff | はい、大丈夫です。 | Hai, daijoubu desu. | Yes, that's fine. |

Notes: the staff speak keigo (なさいます, お召し上がり); you answer in plain polite Japanese, which is exactly right. For takeout, say 持ち帰りで (*mochikaeri de*). 払えます is the potential of 払う (to pay).

### 3. Asking for directions

| Speaker | Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| You | すみません、駅はどこですか。 | Sumimasen, eki wa doko desu ka. | Excuse me, where is the station? |
| Local | 駅ですか。この道をまっすぐ行って、二つ目の信号を右に曲がってください。 | Eki desu ka. Kono michi o massugu itte, futatsume no shingou o migi ni magatte kudasai. | The station? Go straight down this street and turn right at the second traffic light. |
| You | 二つ目の信号を右ですね。遠いですか。 | Futatsume no shingou o migi desu ne. Tooi desu ka. | Right at the second light. Is it far? |
| Local | いいえ、歩いて五分ぐらいです。コンビニの隣にありますよ。 | Iie, aruite gofun gurai desu. Konbini no tonari ni arimasu yo. | No, about five minutes on foot. It's next to a convenience store. |
| You | ありがとうございます。助かりました。 | Arigatou gozaimasu. Tasukarimashita. | Thank you. That really helped. |

Notes: repeating back key information with ね (二つ目の信号を右ですね) is a natural way to confirm. 〜目 (*-me*) after a counter makes an ordinal: 二つ目, the second one.

### 4. Making weekend plans (casual)

| Speaker | Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ken | ねえ、土曜日ひま？ | Nee, doyoubi hima? | Hey, are you free on Saturday? |
| Yui | うん、午後なら大丈夫。どうして？ | Un, gogo nara daijoubu. Doushite? | Yeah, the afternoon's fine. Why? |
| Ken | 新しい映画、見に行かない？ | Atarashii eiga, mi ni ikanai? | Want to go see the new movie? |
| Yui | いいね！何時にどこで会う？ | Ii ne! Nanji ni doko de au? | Sounds good! What time and where shall we meet? |
| Ken | 二時に駅の前で会おう。 | Niji ni eki no mae de aou. | Let's meet in front of the station at two. |
| Yui | 了解。雨が降ったらどうする？ | Ryoukai. Ame ga futtara dou suru? | Got it. What if it rains? |
| Ken | 映画館は駅の中だから、大丈夫だよ。 | Eigakan wa eki no naka da kara, daijoubu da yo. | The cinema's inside the station, so it's fine. |
| Yui | そっか。じゃあ、土曜日ね。 | Sokka. Jaa, doyoubi ne. | Oh, right. See you Saturday, then. |

Notes: casual speech drops は and を (映画、見に行かない？), uses plain forms (行かない, 会おう), and turns questions into rising intonation. うん is a casual "yes". Compare with dialogue 1 to see the same functions (inviting, agreeing) in two registers.

### 5. At the convenience store

| Speaker | Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clerk | いらっしゃいませ。お弁当は温めますか。 | Irasshaimase. O-bentou wa atatamemasu ka. | Welcome. Shall I heat up your bento? |
| You | はい、お願いします。 | Hai, onegaishimasu. | Yes, please. |
| Clerk | 袋はご利用ですか。 | Fukuro wa go-riyou desu ka. | Would you like a bag? |
| You | いいえ、大丈夫です。 | Iie, daijoubu desu. | No, I'm fine. |
| Clerk | ポイントカードはお持ちですか。 | Pointo kaado wa o-mochi desu ka. | Do you have a point card? |
| You | 持っていません。 | Motte imasen. | I don't have one. |
| Clerk | 六百五十円になります。 | Roppyaku gojuu en ni narimasu. | That comes to 650 yen. |
| You | じゃあ、カードで。 | Jaa, kaado de. | By card, then. |
| Clerk | ありがとうございました。 | Arigatou gozaimashita. | Thank you very much. |

## Common mistakes and culture notes

### Mistakes I see in every beginner class

| Mistake | Instead | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 私は... in every sentence | drop it when obvious | repeating 私は sounds self-centered and stiff |
| あなたは学生ですか | 田中さんは学生ですか | あなた can sound distant or even rude; use names |
| ジョンさんです (about yourself) | ジョンです | さん is never used for yourself |
| 静か部屋, きれい花 | 静かな部屋, きれいな花 | na-adjectives need な before nouns |
| 高いでした | 高かったです | i-adjectives carry their own past tense |
| 猫があります | 猫がいます | living things use いる |
| 三つ人 | 三人 | people have their own counter |
| 行いて | 行って | 行く is the te-form exception |
| 知っていません | 知りません | the negative of 知っている is irregular |
| 帰ます | 帰ります | 帰る is a group 1 verb in disguise |
| おばさん for grandmother | おばあさん | vowel length changes the word |
| いいえ as a flat refusal | ちょっと... | direct "no" sounds harsh; see below |

**はい means "that's right", not "yes".** To a negative question, はい confirms the negative. 行かないんですか (*ikanai n desu ka*, aren't you going?) answered with はい means "right, I'm not going". English speakers get this backward for months.

### Culture that lives in the language

- **Aizuchi.** Listeners give constant short feedback: はい, ええ, うん, そうですね, へえ (*hee*, oh really). Silence on the phone makes speakers think you have hung up. Nodding and making small noises while someone talks is polite, not interrupting.
- **Indirect refusals.** A plain いいえ can feel blunt. People trail off instead: 今日はちょっと... (*kyou wa chotto...*, "today is a little...") means no, and nobody needs the rest of the sentence. 考えておきます (*kangaete okimasu*, "I'll think about it") is often a polite no as well.
- **Apologizing and thanking overlap.** すみません can mean "sorry", "excuse me" and "thank you for going to trouble for me". Using it generously is normal.
- **Humility.** When complimented, deflect: いいえ、まだまだです. When talking about your own family or company to outsiders, use the humble terms.
- **Set phrases around meals and homes.** Say いただきます before eating and ごちそうさまでした after; say お邪魔します (*ojama shimasu*, "sorry to intrude") when entering someone's home, and take your shoes off at the entrance (玄関 *genkan*).
- **Wasei eigo (English made in Japan).** Many katakana words look English but mean something else: マンション (*manshon*) is a condominium or apartment building, not a mansion; コンセント (*konsento*) is a power outlet; ノートパソコン (*nooto pasokon*) is a laptop; サラリーマン (*sarariiman*) is a salaried office worker. Pronounce loanwords the Japanese way: マクドナルド (*Makudonarudo*) is how you ask for McDonald's.
- **Quiet public spaces.** Trains are quiet; phone calls on trains are frowned upon. Speaking softly is a form of politeness, and so is not pointing at people.

## A 16-week study plan

### The daily routine (about 2 hours)

| Block | Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| SRS reviews | 20 to 30 min | all Anki reviews first, then 10 to 15 new cards |
| Course work | 40 to 50 min | one textbook lesson section: read, do exercises, read examples aloud |
| Listening | 20 min | beginner podcast or video at your level; shadow 5 lines |
| Reading | 10 to 15 min | graded readers, NHK News Web Easy (from week 8) |
| Output | 10 to 15 min | write 3 to 5 sentences with today's grammar; say them aloud |

Once a week, add a 30-minute **speaking session**: a language exchange partner, a tutor, or at minimum a recorded monologue you listen back to.

### Week by week

| Week | Focus | By the end you can |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hiragana (all), greetings, です sentences | read any hiragana word slowly; introduce yourself |

### Spaced repetition that you will not quit

- **Use Anki's FSRS scheduler** (built in since version 23.10; enable it in the deck options) with desired retention around 90 percent. It schedules reviews more efficiently than the old default.
- **Cap new cards at 10 to 20 a day.** Each new card costs you roughly ten reviews over its lifetime. Twenty new cards a day means around 150 to 200 reviews a day within a month, which is at the limit of a two-hour budget.
- **Reviews before new cards, every day.** If you skip a day, do the reviews and add no new cards until the backlog is gone.
- **Make cards Japanese to English first** (recognition), with audio and an example sentence. Add English to Japanese only for words you want to *say*.
- **Delete leeches.** A card you have failed eight times needs a new mnemonic or a different example sentence, not more repetitions.
- **Mine sentences from what you read and hear.** Words you met in context stick better than list words.

> **EXERCISE:** Plan your week 1 on paper: for each day, list which hiragana columns you will learn (two columns a day covers the basic 46 by day 5, dakuten and yoon on day 6), when your Anki session happens, and one greeting you will say aloud 20 times. *Model answer:* Mon あ, か columns; Tue さ, た; Wed な, は; Thu ま, や, ら; Fri わ, ん + review; Sat dakuten, handakuten, yoon; Sun full review, read 20 words from a children's book or NHK Easy Japanese lesson 1.

- **NHK WORLD-JAPAN Easy Japanese**: 48 free audio-drama lessons with explanations in many languages, plus an app.

- **JF Japanese e-Learning Minato** (Japan Foundation): the free IRODORI Japanese Online Course (self-study, Starter A1 to Pre-Intermediate A2/B1, with interactive dramas) and Marugoto courses; requires a free account.
- **Tofugu**: free guides to hiragana, katakana and many grammar points, plus a large database of learning-resource reviews.

**Vocabulary, kanji and dictionaries**

- **Anki**: free on desktop, Android and the web (AnkiWeb); the iOS app is paid. Use a well-rated shared beginner deck, or build your own from your textbook.
- **Jisho.org**: the go-to free online dictionary, with kanji search by radicals and example sentences.
- **WaniKani**: kanji and vocabulary SRS by radicals; the first three levels are free, the rest is a subscription.
- **Renshuu**: free vocabulary, kanji and grammar drills with an optional paid tier.
- **OJAD** (Online Japanese Accent Dictionary, University of Tokyo): free pitch-accent lookups and a sentence prosody tool.

**Reading and listening**

- **NHK News Web Easy**: short daily news articles in simplified Japanese with toggleable furigana and audio. Try it from about week 8.
- **Tadoku free graded readers** (NPO Tadoku Supporters): over 200 free illustrated stories from level 0 (starter) upward, with furigana.
- **YouTube and podcasts**: *Comprehensible Japanese* (beginner input videos, with a free tier and a paid library), *Nihongo con Teppei for Beginners* (short podcast episodes), *Japanese Ammo with Misa* (grammar explanations in English).

**Speaking**

- **HelloTalk** and **Tandem**: language-exchange apps with free tiers; you teach English and practice Japanese with native speakers.
- Local Japanese conversation meetups, university language tables, and Japan Foundation events if you live near a branch.

**Testing**

- **JLPT** (jlpt.jp): free official sample questions for every level. In 2026 the tests are held on Sunday 5 July and Sunday 6 December; many overseas sites run only the December sitting, so check your local host institution for registration windows. Registration often closes months before the test date.

**What comes after this guide**

Once you are comfortable with everything here, the path to N3 (intermediate, about B1) is mostly volume: more kanji (toward 650), more vocabulary (toward 3,500), and hours of reading and listening to real content. Graduate from textbooks to graded readers, then to manga with furigana, slice-of-life anime and dramas with Japanese subtitles, and YouTubers who speak clearly. Keep your Anki habit, keep a weekly speaking session, and keep the polite form as your default. がんばってください (*ganbatte kudasai*): do your best, and enjoy it.
