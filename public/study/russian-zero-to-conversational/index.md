Russian is spoken by more than 250 million people, it is the language of Tolstoy, Bulgakov and a huge share of the world's science, chess and space history, and it is still a lingua franca across much of Eastern Europe and Central Asia. It also has a reputation as a hard language, and that is partly true: six cases, verbs that come in pairs, and a new alphabet. The good news is that the alphabet takes a week, the grammar is regular enough to learn from tables, and every one of those "hard" features has a simple core that covers most real conversation. This module takes you from zero to a conversational, high-school level (roughly CEFR A2, reaching toward B1): reading anything aloud, handling everyday situations, talking about yourself, your family, your plans and your past, and understanding slow, clear speech.

Each section ends with an exercise and its answers. Say every Russian example out loud. You learn a language with your mouth and ears, not only your eyes.

## How to learn Russian, and how long it takes

### What "hard" actually means

The US Foreign Service Institute (FSI) puts Russian in its group of "hard languages" for English speakers: about 44 weeks, or roughly 1,100 classroom hours plus homework, to reach *professional working proficiency*. That is a diplomat who negotiates in Russian, far beyond the goal here. For conversational A2, a realistic budget is about **200-300 hours** of focused study and practice; low B1 is closer to **400-500 hours**. These are rules of thumb, not guarantees: people who already know another inflected language (German, Latin, Polish) move faster.

At one focused hour a day, that means:

| Target | Can do | Rough hours | At ~1 h/day |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Read Cyrillic, introduce yourself, order food, ask prices and directions | 100-150 | 3-5 months |
| A2 | Talk about family, work, school, hobbies, past and future plans; simple phone calls | 200-300 | 7-10 months |
| B1 | Follow slow podcasts, tell a story, explain opinions, travel without English | 400-500 | 12-18 months |

The 16-week plan at the end of this module runs at about 8-10 hours a week and gets you to a solid A1 and well into A2. After that, it is volume: more listening, more reading, more speaking.

### Why it feels hard, and why it is fine

- **The alphabet.** Looks alien, is actually easy. About a week of daily practice.
- **Cases.** Nouns change their endings depending on their job in the sentence. English does this with pronouns only (*he / him / his*); Russian does it with every noun and adjective. You will learn them one at a time, each with a clear purpose.
- **Aspect.** Most verbs come in pairs: one for a process, one for a completed result. It sounds abstract; in practice it maps onto questions you already ask in English ("was I reading, or did I finish reading?").
- **Stress.** Word stress is unpredictable and changes vowel sounds. You learn it with each word, the way you learn spelling in English.

What makes Russian *easier* than you expect: no articles (no *a* / *the*), no verb "to be" in the present tense, only three tenses, flexible word order, and a writing system that is close to phonetic once you know a handful of rules.

### Method: what actually works

1. **Input first, lots of it.** Listen daily to material you mostly understand (graded podcasts, learner YouTube). Comprehension drives acquisition.
2. **Spaced repetition for vocabulary.** A flashcard program such as Anki schedules each card just before you would forget it. Ten minutes a day beats an hour on Sunday. Put **sentences** on cards, not isolated words, and always include the stress mark and audio.
3. **Learn words in their grammatical context.** Not *книга = book*, but *книга (f), нет книги, пять книг*. Not *читать = to read*, but *читать (impf) / прочитать (pf), читаю, читаешь*.
4. **Speak from week one.** Shadow audio (repeat it a half-second behind the speaker), talk to yourself, and book a conversation partner or tutor by week six.
5. **Tables are tools, not goals.** Look at the tables in this module, then practice with sentences until you stop needing them.

> **TIP:** Set up your keyboard now. On Windows add "Russian" (or "Russian - Mnemonic", which maps letters to similar-sounding Latin keys) under Settings > Time & language > Language & region; on macOS add "Russian - Phonetic"; on phones add a Russian keyboard. Typing in Cyrillic from day one is the fastest way to lock in the alphabet.

> **EXERCISE:** Write down (1) how many hours per week you can honestly commit, (2) the date you start, and (3) one concrete reason you want Russian (a trip, a friend, a book). Using the table above, estimate the month you will reach A1 and A2.
>
> **Answers:** There is no single answer. As a check: 7 hours a week reaches ~120 hours (A1) in about 4 months and ~250 hours (A2) in about 8 months. If your estimate is much faster than that, you are probably counting passive time (background audio) as focused study.

## The Cyrillic alphabet

Russian uses the **Cyrillic** alphabet: 33 letters, 10 vowels, 21 consonants and 2 signs that have no sound of their own. It is not a code to be deciphered; it is a phonetic alphabet that happens to share ancestors (Greek) with ours. The fastest way in is to sort the letters by how familiar they already are.

![Thirty-three Cyrillic letters grouped into four sets: true friends, false friends, new shapes with familiar sounds, and new sounds](study/russian-zero-to-conversational/cyrillic-map.svg "Cyrillic sorted by familiarity: 5 free letters, 7 traps, 13 new shapes, 8 new sounds")

### Four groups

**1. True friends (look and sound like English):** А а (a), К к (k), М м (m), О о (o), Т т (t).

**2. False friends (look English, sound different):** these are the traps.

| Letter | Looks like | Actually sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| В в | B | **v** | вода́ (voda) - water |
| Е е | E | **ye** as in *yes* | нет (net) - no |
| Н н | H | **n** | нос (nos) - nose |
| Р р | P | rolled **r** | рот (rot) - mouth |
| С с | C | **s** | сок (sok) - juice |
| У у | y | **oo** as in *boot* | суп (sup) - soup |
| Х х | X | **kh**, like Scottish *loch* | хлеб (khleb) - bread |

The classic example: РЕСТОРАН is not "pectopah", it is *restoran*, restaurant.

**3. New shapes, familiar sounds:** Б (b), Г (g), Д (d), З (z), И (ee), Й (short y), Л (l), П (p), Ф (f), Э (e as in *met*), Ю (yu), Я (ya), Ё (yo).

**4. Genuinely new sounds:** Ж (zh), Ц (ts), Ч (ch), Ш (sh), Щ (shch), Ы (y, a vowel English lacks), Ъ (hard sign), Ь (soft sign).

### Handwriting and italics

Russians write in cursive, and some cursive and italic letters look nothing like their print forms: italic *т* looks like a Latin *m*, italic *д* looks like *g*, and italic *и* looks like *u*. You do not need to write cursive to start, but learn to *read* italics early, because textbooks and signs use them. A good week-two project: copy the cursive alphabet from a free chart and write your name.

## Pronunciation: stress, reduction, and soft consonants

Russian spelling is close to phonetic, but not identical. Five rules explain almost every gap between how a word is written and how it is said.

### Rule 1: stress is free and it matters

Any syllable can be stressed, and there is no rule that predicts it. Stress can even move between forms of the same word: го́род (gorod, city) but города́ (goroda, cities). Sometimes stress is the only difference between two words: за́мок (zamok) - castle, замо́к (zamok) - lock. Learn stress with every new word; a wrong stress confuses listeners more than a wrong ending.

### Rule 2: unstressed vowels shrink

Only the stressed vowel is pronounced fully. Unstressed vowels are reduced:

- **Unstressed О sounds like А** (this is called *akanye*). молоко́ (moloko, milk) is said [malako]; хорошо́ (khorosho, good) is [kharasho]; спаси́бо is [spasiba].
- **Unstressed Е and Я sound like a short И** (*ikanye*). сестра́ (sestra, sister) is [sistra]; язы́к (yazyk, language) is [yizyk]; сейча́с (seychas, now) is [sichas].

![Hard and soft vowel pairs, and how unstressed vowels reduce](study/russian-zero-to-conversational/vowel-pairs.svg "Five vowel pairs mark a consonant as hard or soft; unstressed o and e shrink")

### Rule 3: hard and soft consonants

Most Russian consonants come in two versions: **hard** (plain, like English) and **soft** (palatalized: said with the middle of the tongue raised toward the roof of your mouth, as if a faint *y* were starting). Russian does not have separate letters for soft consonants. Instead, the **vowel after the consonant** signals which one you get:

| Hard-signaling vowel | а | э | ы | о | у |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Soft-signaling vowel** | **я** | **е** | **и** | **ё** | **ю** |

So in мат (mat, checkmate) the *т* is hard, and in мать (mat', mother) the soft sign makes the *т* soft. Minimal pairs are worth practicing: лук (luk, onion) vs люк (lyuk, hatch); быть (byt', to be) vs бить (bit', to hit); брат (brat, brother) vs брать (brat', to take).

Three consonants are **always hard** (ж, ш, ц) and three are **always soft** (ч, щ, й), whatever vowel follows. That is why жизнь (zhizn', life) is said with a hard *ж*, closer to [zhyzn'].

### Rule 4: the two signs

- **Ь, the soft sign,** makes the preceding consonant soft: день (den', day), ночь (noch', night), учи́тель (uchitel', teacher). Before a vowel it also adds a *y* glide: семья́ (sem'ya, family) is [sem-ya], not [se-mya].
- **Ъ, the hard sign,** is rare. It separates a prefix from a following е, ё, ю, я, so you hear the *y*: съесть (s"yest', to eat up), объявле́ние (ob"yavleniye, announcement).

### Rule 5: voicing assimilation

Six consonant pairs differ only in voicing: б/п, в/ф, г/к, д/т, ж/ш, з/с.

- **At the end of a word, voiced consonants go voiceless:** хлеб (khleb) is [khlep], год (god) is [got], друг (drug, friend) is [druk], муж (muzh, husband) is [mush].
- **In a cluster, the last consonant decides:** во́дка is [votka], ска́зка (skazka, fairy tale) is [skaska], and вокза́л (vokzal, train station) is [vagzal], because the voiced *з* makes the *к* voiced too.
- **Prepositions join the next word:** в шко́ле (v shkole, at school) is [fshkole].

A few spellings you simply memorize: что (chto, what) is [shto]; коне́чно (konechno, of course) is [kanyeshna]; the ending -ого/-его is [-ava]/[-yeva], so его́ (yego, him) is [yivo] and сего́дня (segodnya, today) is [sivodnya]; the first в in здра́вствуйте is silent.

> **TIP:** Do not try to fake a Russian accent from rules. Pick a 30-second clip with a transcript, listen five times, then shadow it (speak along a half-second behind) until you can match the rhythm. Rhythm and stress carry more meaning than perfect vowels.

### Ты and вы: the most important social choice

Russian has two words for *you*:

- **ты (ty)** - singular and informal: family, friends, children, pets, and people your own age in casual settings (students with students, for example).
- **вы (vy)** - plural (always), and the polite singular: strangers, shop staff, officials, teachers, older people, colleagues you do not know well.

Default to **вы** with any adult you do not know. The switch to **ты** is usually offered by the older or higher-status person: Дава́й на ты? (Davay na ty? - Shall we say *ty*?). With **вы**, adults are traditionally addressed by first name plus patronymic (a middle name formed from the father's name): Ива́н Петро́вич (Ivan Petrovich, "Ivan, son of Pyotr"), А́нна Серге́евна (Anna Sergeyevna). Teachers at schools and universities are almost always addressed this way.

Among friends, Russians use short forms of names: Алекса́ндр becomes Са́ша (Sasha), Мари́я becomes Ма́ша (Masha), Дми́трий becomes Ди́ма (Dima), Екатери́на becomes Ка́тя (Katya). Using the short form with a stranger is as odd as calling your new boss "Bobby".

> **NOTE:** Every verb, imperative and many phrases change with ты / вы: извини́ / извини́те, как тебя́ зову́т / как вас зову́т, у тебя́ / у вас. When in doubt, the longer, -те form is the polite one.

> **EXERCISE:** Which form would you use, and what would you say? (1) Greeting a professor in the morning. (2) Asking a friend's younger brother his name. (3) Saying sorry after bumping into a stranger. (4) Saying bye to a classmate.
>
> **Answers:** (1) вы: До́брое у́тро! or Здра́вствуйте! (plus the professor's name and patronymic) (2) ты: Как тебя́ зову́т? (3) вы: Извини́те! (4) ты: Пока́!

## Building sentences: pronouns, gender, questions, negation

### Personal pronouns

| Russian | Translit. | English |
|---|---|---|
| я | ya | I |
| ты | ty | you (informal singular) |
| он | on | he / it (masculine noun) |
| она́ | ona | she / it (feminine noun) |
| оно́ | ono | it (neuter noun) |
| мы | my | we |
| вы | vy | you (plural or polite) |
| они́ | oni | they |

Because every noun has a gender, *it* depends on the noun: стол (stol, table) is masculine, so "where is the table?" is answered with Он здесь (On zdes', it is here).

### No "to be" in the present

Russian drops *am / is / are* in the present tense. There are also no articles. So:

- Я студе́нт. (Ya student.) - I am a student.
- Он врач. (On vrach.) - He is a doctor.
- Э́то моя́ кни́га. (Eto moya kniga.) - This is my book.
- Где метро́? (Gde metro?) - Where is the subway?

In writing, a dash replaces the missing *is* when both sides are nouns: Москва́ - столи́ца Росси́и (Moskva - stolitsa Rossii, Moscow is the capital of Russia).

The word **есть** (yest', "there is / exists") does appear when you assert existence or possession: У меня́ есть брат. (U menya yest' brat.) - I have a brother (literally "by me there is a brother"). You will meet this structure again with the genitive case.

### Grammatical gender

Every noun is **masculine, feminine or neuter**, and you can almost always tell from the ending of its dictionary form:

| Gender | Typical endings | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Masculine | consonant, -й, some -ь | стол (stol) - table, музе́й (muzey) - museum, слова́рь (slovar') - dictionary |
| Feminine | -а, -я, some -ь | ма́ма (mama) - mom, неде́ля (nedelya) - week, тетра́дь (tetrad') - notebook |
| Neuter | -о, -е, -мя | окно́ (okno) - window, мо́ре (more) - sea, и́мя (imya) - name |

Two traps:

- **Words in -ь** can be masculine or feminine; learn the gender with the word. Nouns in **-ость** (-ost') are always feminine: ра́дость (radost', joy).
- **Male people ending in -а / -я** are masculine: па́па (papa, dad), дя́дя (dyadya, uncle), мужчи́на (muzhchina, man), and male nicknames like Са́ша, Ди́ма. Adjectives and past-tense verbs agree with them as masculine.

### Plurals (nominative)

| Singular ending | Plural ending | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| consonant (m) | -ы | стол > столы́ (stoly), студе́нт > студе́нты |
| -й, -ь (m/f) | -и | музе́й > музе́и, тетра́дь > тетра́ди |

Common irregulars you meet in week one: брат > бра́тья (brat'ya, brothers), друг > друзья́ (druz'ya, friends), челове́к > лю́ди (lyudi, people), ребёнок > де́ти (deti, children), дом > дома́ (doma, houses), го́род > города́ (goroda, cities).

### The spelling rules

Three spelling rules override endings everywhere in the language. Learn them now and half of the "exceptions" in later tables disappear:

1. After **г, к, х, ж, ш, щ, ч**, write **и**, never ы: кни́га > кни́ги (knigi), not *книгы*.
2. After **г, к, х, ж, ш, щ, ч, ц**, write **а, у**, never я, ю: слы́шать > слы́шат (they hear), not *слышят*.
3. After **ж, ш, щ, ч, ц**, write unstressed **е** instead of о: с му́жем (s muzhem, with the husband), not *с мужом*.

### Asking questions

A yes/no question is just a statement with a rising pitch on the key word: Ты студе́нт. (You are a student.) > Ты студе́нт? (Are you a student?). No word order change, no auxiliary.

| како́й | kakoy | which, what kind of (changes like an adjective) |
| чей | chey | whose (changes like an adjective) |

Russian keeps **где** (location) and **куда́** (destination) strictly apart: Где ты? (Where are you?) but Куда́ ты идёшь? (Where are you going?). English "where" hides the difference; Russian grammar depends on it.

### Negation

Put **не** (ne) directly in front of the word you negate, usually the verb: Я не зна́ю. (I don't know.) Я не студе́нт. (I am not a student.)

Russian likes **double negatives**. Negative words need не on the verb too:

- Я никогда́ не был в Москве́. (Ya nikogda ne byl v Moskve.) - I have never been to Moscow.
- Никто́ не звони́л. (Nikto ne zvonil.) - Nobody called.
- Я ничего́ не зна́ю. (Ya nichego ne znayu.) - I don't know anything.

> **EXERCISE:** (A) Give the gender: кни́га, дом, окно́, пла́тье, день, ночь, па́па, пи́во. (B) Make plural: студе́нт, ру́чка, письмо́, музе́й, друг. (C) Translate: "Where is the bank?", "I am not a teacher.", "Nobody knows."
>
> **Answers:** (A) кни́га f, дом m, окно́ n, пла́тье (plat'ye, dress) n, день (den', day) m, ночь (noch', night) f, па́па m, пи́во (pivo, beer) n. (B) студе́нты, ру́чки (spelling rule 1), пи́сьма (pis'ma, letters), музе́и, друзья́. (C) Где банк? / Я не учи́тель. / Никто́ не зна́ет.

## Verbs in the present tense

Russian verbs have one present tense that covers English *I read*, *I am reading* and *I have been reading (for an hour)*. Almost every verb follows one of **two conjugation patterns**. Find the pattern and you can conjugate verbs you have never seen.

### First and second conjugation

| Person | 1st conjugation: чита́ть (to read) | 2nd conjugation: говори́ть (to speak) |
|---|---|---|
| я | чита́**ю** (chitayu) | говор**ю́** (govoryu) |
| ты | чита́**ешь** (chitayesh') | говор**и́шь** (govorish') |
| он / она́ | чита́**ет** (chitayet) | говор**и́т** (govorit) |
| мы | чита́**ем** (chitayem) | говор**и́м** (govorim) |
| вы | чита́**ете** (chitayete) | говор**и́те** (govorite) |
| они́ | чита́**ют** (chitayut) | говор**я́т** (govoryat) |

The signature is the vowel in the middle forms: **е** for the first conjugation, **и** for the second. The *they* form ends in -ут/-ют (1st) or -ат/-ят (2nd). When the ending is stressed, е becomes ё: жить (zhit', to live) > живу́, живёшь, живёт, живём, живёте, живу́т.

**Which pattern?** Most verbs in **-ить** are 2nd conjugation, plus a short list of -еть and -ать verbs you learn as exceptions (ви́деть - to see, смотре́ть - to watch, слы́шать - to hear, спать - to sleep, держа́ть - to hold). Almost everything else is 1st conjugation.

### Stem changes you will see a lot

- **2nd conjugation, я-form only:** a consonant shifts. люби́ть (to love) > люблю́, лю́бишь; ви́деть > ви́жу, ви́дишь; спать > сплю, спишь.
- **-овать / -евать verbs** swap the suffix for -у-: рисова́ть (to draw) > рису́ю, рису́ешь; танцева́ть (to dance) > танцу́ю, танцу́ешь.
- **-авать verbs** drop -ва-: дава́ть (to give) > даю́, даёшь; встава́ть (to get up) > встаю́, встаёшь.
- **Some 1st conjugation verbs change the stem throughout:** писа́ть (to write) > пишу́, пи́шешь, пи́шет, пи́шем, пи́шете, пи́шут.

### Five irregulars worth memorizing now

| | хоте́ть (to want) | мочь (can) | есть (to eat) | идти́ (to go on foot) | е́хать (to go by vehicle) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| я | хочу́ | могу́ | ем | иду́ | е́ду |
| ты | хо́чешь | мо́жешь | ешь | идёшь | е́дешь |
| он/она́ | хо́чет | мо́жет | ест | идёт | е́дет |
| мы | хоти́м | мо́жем | еди́м | идём | е́дем |
| вы | хоти́те | мо́жете | еди́те | идёте | е́дете |
| они́ | хотя́т | мо́гут | едя́т | иду́т | е́дут |

хоте́ть is a hybrid: 1st-conjugation singular, 2nd-conjugation plural.

### Reflexive verbs in -ся

Verbs ending in **-ся** (-sya) conjugate normally and then add **-сь** after a vowel or **-ся** after a consonant: занима́ться (to study, work out) > я занима́юсь, ты занима́ешься, он занима́ется, мы занима́емся, вы занима́етесь, они́ занима́ются. Note that -тся and -ться are both pronounced [-tsa].

### Study verbs: four ways to say "study"

English "study" splits four ways in Russian, and learners mix them up constantly:

| Verb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| учи́ть + accusative | to learn or memorize something | Я учу́ ру́сский язы́к. (Ya uchu russkiy yazyk.) - I am learning Russian. |
| учи́ться + where | to be a student somewhere | Я учу́сь в университе́те. (Ya uchus' v universitete.) - I study at a university. |
| изуча́ть + accusative | to study a subject in depth | Она́ изуча́ет исто́рию. (Ona izuchayet istoriyu.) - She studies history. |
| занима́ться | to do homework, to practice | Ве́чером я занима́юсь. (Vecherom ya zanimayus'.) - In the evening I study. |

> **EXERCISE:** Conjugate in the present: (1) я (понима́ть) (2) они́ (говори́ть) (3) ты (жить) (4) мы (хоте́ть) (5) она́ (писа́ть) (6) вы (смотре́ть) (7) я (люби́ть) (8) он (занима́ться)
>
> **Answers:** (1) понима́ю (2) говоря́т (3) живёшь (4) хоти́м (5) пи́шет (6) смо́трите (7) люблю́ (8) занима́ется

## The six cases, one at a time

A **case** is a set of endings that tells you a noun's job in the sentence. English uses word order and prepositions for this ("the dog bit the man" vs "the man bit the dog"). Russian uses endings, which is why its word order is so flexible: Ма́ма лю́бит па́пу and Па́пу лю́бит ма́ма both mean "Mom loves Dad"; the -у on па́пу marks him as the one being loved.

There are six cases. Each one answers its own question words, which is the easiest way to remember what it is for:

![Six cases arranged around a wheel, each with its question words, main uses and a sample ending](study/russian-zero-to-conversational/case-wheel.svg "Each case answers its own questions: learn the question, and the ending follows")

| Case | Questions | Core job | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | кто? что? | subject, dictionary form | **Ма́ма** до́ма. - Mom is home. |
| Accusative | кого́? что? куда́? | direct object, destination | Я ви́жу **ма́му**. - I see Mom. |
| Genitive | кого́? чего́? отку́да? | "of", absence, quantity | Э́то дом **ма́мы**. - This is Mom's house. |
| Dative | кому́? чему́? | "to / for" a person, age, likes | Я звоню́ **ма́ме**. - I call Mom. |
| Instrumental | кем? чем? с кем? | "with", "by means of", roles | Я гуля́ю с **ма́мой**. - I walk with Mom. |
| Prepositional | где? о ком? о чём? | location, "about" | Я ду́маю о **ма́ме**. - I think about Mom. |

Learn them in this order: **prepositional** (the easiest, and you need "where" on day one), then **accusative**, **genitive**, **dative**, **instrumental**. That is the order used below.

### Prepositional: where and about

Used after **в** (in, at), **на** (on, at) for location, and **о / об** (about).

**Endings:** almost everything takes **-е**. Feminine nouns in -ь, and nouns in -ия, -ие, -ий take **-и**.

| Nominative | Prepositional | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Москва́ | в Москве́ | in Moscow |
| стол | на столе́ | on the table |
| музе́й | в музе́е | in the museum |
| письмо́ | в письме́ | in the letter |
| тетра́дь | в тетра́ди | in the notebook |
| Росси́я | в Росси́и | in Russia |
| зда́ние | в зда́нии | in the building |

A dozen common masculine nouns take a stressed **-у́** for location: в лесу́ (in the forest), в саду́ (in the garden), на полу́ (on the floor), в аэропорту́ (at the airport), в шкафу́ (in the wardrobe), на мосту́ (on the bridge), в году́ (in the year). About them you still say о ле́се, о са́де.

**в or на?** в means "inside" and is the default. на is used for surfaces, open spaces, events, and a list of places you memorize:

| на + prepositional | Meaning |
|---|---|
| на рабо́те | at work |
| на уро́ке, на ле́кции | in class, at a lecture |
| на конце́рте | at a concert |
| на у́лице | outside, on the street |
| на по́чте | at the post office |
| на вокза́ле, на ста́нции | at the train station, at the station |
| на ры́нке | at the market |
| на ку́хне | in the kitchen |

**About:** Мы говори́м о фи́льме. (My govorim o fil'me.) - We are talking about the film. Before a vowel sound, о becomes об: об э́том (ob etom) - about this.

### Accusative: the direct object and destinations

The accusative marks the thing you act on. Only **two** changes to learn in the singular:

1. Feminine **-а > -у**, **-я > -ю**: Я чита́ю кни́гу. (Ya chitayu knigu.) - I am reading a book. Я люблю́ Росси́ю.
2. **Animate masculine** nouns (people and animals) take the genitive ending **-а / -я**: Я ви́жу бра́та. (Ya vizhu brata.) - I see my brother. Я жду учи́теля. (Ya zhdu uchitelya.) - I am waiting for the teacher.

Everything else stays in the nominative form: Я ви́жу стол, окно́, тетра́дь. Masculine words that end in -а behave like feminine ones: Я люблю́ ма́му и па́пу.

Other uses:

- **Destination** after в / на (answering куда́?): Я иду́ в шко́лу. (I am going to school.) Мы е́дем на рабо́ту. (We are going to work.) Compare location: Я в шко́ле. (I am at school.)
- **Days of the week** with в: в пя́тницу (on Friday), в суббо́ту (on Saturday).
- **через + time** = "in / after": через неде́лю (in a week).

### Genitive: of, from, none, and how many

The genitive is the most frequent oblique case. Learn its five jobs:

1. **"Of" and possession:** дом бра́та (dom brata) - my brother's house; ча́шка ча́я (chashka chaya) - a cup of tea; центр го́рода - the city center.
2. **Absence with нет:** У меня́ нет вре́мени. (U menya net vremeni.) - I don't have time. Здесь нет ба́нка. - There is no bank here.
3. **Quantity:** мно́го (a lot), ма́ло (little), ско́лько (how many), не́сколько (several) + genitive plural: мно́го книг, ско́лько люде́й.
4. **After numbers** (see the numbers section).
5. **After prepositions:** у (at someone's, by), из (out of, from a place), с (from a surface or event; also since), от (from a person), до (until, up to), без (without), для (for), по́сле (after), о́коло (near).

**Singular endings:** masculine and neuter **-а / -я**; feminine **-ы / -и** (spelling rule 1 applies).

| Nominative | Genitive |
|---|---|
| брат, музе́й | бра́та, музе́я |
| окно́, мо́ре | окна́, мо́ря |
| ма́ма, кни́га | ма́мы, кни́ги |
| неде́ля, тетра́дь | неде́ли, тетра́ди |

**Possession with у.** "I have" is У меня́ (есть) ...; "I don't have" is У меня́ нет + genitive:

- У меня́ есть сестра́. (U menya yest' sestra.) - I have a sister.
- У меня́ нет сестры́. (U menya net sestry.) - I don't have a sister.
- У бра́та есть маши́на. - My brother has a car.

**Fleeting vowels.** Some masculine nouns lose an о or е when an ending is added: оте́ц (father) > отца́, день (day) > дня, пода́рок (gift) > пода́рка. Dictionaries show the genitive for exactly this reason.

**Genitive plural** is the one hard form. Rules of thumb: masculine hard stem **-ов** (столо́в), -й stem **-ев** (музе́ев), -ь / ж / ш / ч / щ stems **-ей** (рубле́й, враче́й); feminine and neuter drop their ending (**zero ending**): книг, мам, мест, неде́ль, sometimes adding a vowel to break a cluster: окно́ > о́кон, де́вушка > де́вушек.

### Dative: to whom, for whom

**Endings:** masculine and neuter **-у / -ю**; feminine **-е** (or **-и** for -ь and -ия nouns).

| Nominative | Dative |
|---|---|
| брат, учи́тель | бра́ту, учи́телю |
| ма́ма, сестра́ | ма́ме, сестре́ |
| Мари́я, тетра́дь | Мари́и, тетра́ди |

**Uses:**

- **Indirect object** (giving, telling, showing, writing *to* someone): Я пишу́ письмо́ ма́ме. - I am writing a letter to Mom. Also verbs that take the dative in Russian but not in English: звони́ть (to call): Я звоню́ дру́гу. помога́ть (to help): Я помога́ю бра́ту.
- **Liking:** нра́виться (to please). The thing liked is the subject; the person is dative: Мне нра́вится Москва́. (Mne nravitsya Moskva.) - I like Moscow (literally "Moscow is pleasing to me"). Plural thing, plural verb: Мне нра́вятся ко́шки. - I like cats.
- **Age:** Мне два́дцать лет. (Mne dvadtsat' let.) - I am twenty. Бра́ту три́дцать лет. - My brother is thirty.
- **Need, must, may:** Мне ну́жно рабо́тать. - I need to work. Вам мо́жно. - You may. Здесь нельзя́ кури́ть. - No smoking here.
- **How someone feels:** Мне хо́лодно. - I am cold. Ему́ ску́чно. - He is bored.
- **Prepositions:** к (towards, to someone's place): Я иду́ к врачу́. - I am going to the doctor's. по (along, by, via): по у́лице (along the street), по телефо́ну (on the phone).

> **NOTE:** мать (mother) and дочь (daughter) insert -ер- in every case except nominative and accusative singular: ма́тери, до́чери. You will usually hear ма́ма instead of мать in conversation anyway.

### Instrumental: with, by, as

**Endings:** masculine and neuter **-ом / -ем** (-ём when stressed); feminine **-ой / -ей**, and **-ью** for -ь nouns.

| Nominative | Instrumental |
|---|---|
| брат, учи́тель, слова́рь | бра́том, учи́телем, словарём |
| молоко́ | молоко́м |
| ма́ма, Та́ня | ма́мой, Та́ней |
| тетра́дь, мать | тетра́дью, ма́терью |

**Uses:**

- **With (together):** с + instrumental. Я гуля́ю с соба́кой. - I am walking the dog. Чай с молоко́м. - Tea with milk.
- **By means of (tool):** no preposition. Я пишу́ ру́чкой. - I write with a pen.
- **Roles and professions** after рабо́тать, быть (past/future), стать: Я рабо́таю программи́стом. - I work as a programmer. Он хо́чет стать врачо́м. - He wants to become a doctor.
- **Hobbies and interests:** занима́ться (to do, practice), интересова́ться (to be interested in), увлека́ться (to be into): Я занима́юсь спо́ртом. - I do sports. Она́ интересу́ется исто́рией. - She is interested in history.
- **Position prepositions:** над (above), под (under), пе́ред (in front of), за (behind), ме́жду (between), ря́дом с (next to): под столо́м, за до́мом, ря́дом с метро́.
- **Times of day and seasons** (as frozen adverbs): у́тром (in the morning), днём (in the afternoon), ве́чером (in the evening), но́чью (at night); зимо́й, весно́й, ле́том, о́сенью (in winter, spring, summer, autumn).

Singular nouns:

| Case | стол (m) | музе́й (m) | окно́ (n) | кни́га (f) | неде́ля (f) | тетра́дь (f) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nom | стол | музе́й | окно́ | кни́га | неде́ля | тетра́дь |
| Acc | стол | музе́й | окно́ | кни́гу | неде́лю | тетра́дь |

### Personal pronouns in all cases

| Nom | я | ты | он / оно́ | она́ | мы | вы | они́ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acc / Gen | меня́ | тебя́ | его́ | её | нас | вас | их |
| Dat | мне | тебе́ | ему́ | ей | нам | вам | им |
| Inst | мной | тобо́й | им | ей | на́ми | ва́ми | и́ми |

## Adjectives, possessives and "this"

Adjectives agree with their noun in **gender, number and case**. The endings are longer than noun endings, but they are extremely regular.

### Dictionary forms and the three types

| Type | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hard: но́вый (new) | но́вый | но́вая | но́вое | но́вые |
| Stressed ending: молодо́й (young) | молодо́й | молода́я | молодо́е | молоды́е |
| Soft: си́ний (dark blue) | си́ний | си́няя | си́нее | си́ние |
| After к, г, х: ру́сский (Russian) | ру́сский | ру́сская | ру́сское | ру́сские |
| After ж, ш, ч, щ: хоро́ший (good) | хоро́ший | хоро́шая | хоро́шее | хоро́шие |

The last two rows are not new patterns; they are the hard pattern bent by the spelling rules.

### Adjectives in all cases

| Case | Masculine | Neuter | Feminine | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nom | но́вый | но́вое | но́вая | но́вые |
| Acc | = nom or gen | но́вое | но́вую | = nom or gen |
| Gen | но́вого | но́вого | но́вой | но́вых |
| Dat | но́вому | но́вому | но́вой | но́вым |
| Inst | но́вым | но́вым | но́вой | но́выми |
| Prep | о но́вом | о но́вом | о но́вой | о но́вых |

Remember that -ого is pronounced [-ava]: но́вого is [novava]. The feminine is a gift: after the nominative and accusative, every case is **-ой**.

In action:

- Э́то мой но́вый дом. - This is my new house.
- Я живу́ в но́вом до́ме. - I live in a new house.
- У меня́ нет но́вой маши́ны. - I don't have a new car.
- Я говорю́ с но́вым дру́гом. - I am talking with a new friend.

### Possessives

| | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| my | мой | моя́ | моё | мои́ |
| your (ты) | твой | твоя́ | твоё | твои́ |
| our | наш | на́ша | на́ше | на́ши |
| your (вы) | ваш | ва́ша | ва́ше | ва́ши |

His, her and their never change: **его́** (yego), **её** (yeyo), **их** (ikh). его́ брат, его́ сестра́, в его́ до́ме.

мой declines like an adjective with soft endings: моего́, моему́, мои́м, о моём; feminine мою́ (accusative) and мое́й (all other oblique cases): Я звоню́ мое́й сестре́.

The reflexive possessive **свой** (svoy, "one's own") replaces мой / твой / его́ / её when the owner is the subject of the clause. Он лю́бит свою́ жену́ means he loves his own wife; Он лю́бит его́ жену́ means he loves *another man's* wife. Worth getting right.

### This and that

э́тот (m), э́та (f), э́то (n), э́ти (pl) mean *this / these*; тот, та, то, те mean *that / those*. Note two different э́то's:

- Э́то моя́ кни́га. - **This is** my book. (э́то as an unchanging "this is")
- Э́та кни́га моя́. - **This book** is mine. (э́та agreeing with кни́га)

### Short forms and comparisons

A few adjectives have **short forms** used after the subject, and you need them early: рад / ра́да / ра́ды (glad), до́лжен / должна́ / должны́ (must), гото́в / гото́ва / гото́вы (ready), за́нят / занята́ / за́няты (busy). Я ра́да вас ви́деть. - I am glad to see you (said by a woman). Я до́лжен идти́. - I have to go (said by a man).

For comparisons, learn a handful of forms whole: бо́льше (bigger, more), ме́ньше (smaller, less), лу́чше (better), ху́же (worse), доро́же (more expensive), деше́вле (cheaper). Than is **чем**: Москва́ бо́льше, чем Каза́нь. - Moscow is bigger than Kazan.

> **EXERCISE:** Make the adjective agree. (1) (краси́вый) го́род (2) в (большо́й) до́ме (3) с (ру́сский) дру́гом (4) Я куплю́ (но́вый) маши́ну. (5) у (моя́) сестры́ (6) (си́ний) мо́ре (7) Он лю́бит (свой) рабо́ту.
>
> **Answers:** (1) краси́вый го́род (2) в большо́м до́ме (3) с ру́сским дру́гом (4) но́вую маши́ну (5) у мое́й сестры́ (6) си́нее мо́ре (7) свою́ рабо́ту

## Past, future and aspect

### The past tense: gender, not person

The past tense is the easiest thing in Russian grammar. Take the infinitive, drop **-ть**, add **-л** plus a gender/number ending. It does not change for *I / you / he*; it changes for masculine / feminine / neuter / plural:

| Subject | чита́ть | говори́ть | быть (to be) |
|---|---|---|---|
| он, я (man), ты (man) | чита́л | говори́л | был |
| она́, я (woman), ты (woman) | чита́ла | говори́ла | была́ |
| оно́ | чита́ло | говори́ло | бы́ло |
| мы, вы, они́ | чита́ли | говори́ли | бы́ли |

So a man says Я чита́л (Ya chital, I read / was reading) and a woman says Я чита́ла. Вы always takes the plural form, even for one person: Вы бы́ли в Москве́? - Have you been to Moscow?

Reflexive verbs add -ся after a consonant, -сь after a vowel: он занима́лся, она́ занима́лась, они́ занима́лись.

A few irregular pasts: идти́ > шёл, шла, шло, шли; есть > ел, е́ла, е́ли; мочь > мог, могла́, могли́.

With **быть**, negation pulls the stress onto не except in the feminine: не́ был, не была́, не́ было, не́ были.

### The future of imperfective verbs

Use the future of быть plus an infinitive:

| я бу́ду | ты бу́дешь | он / она́ бу́дет | мы бу́дем | вы бу́дете | они́ бу́дут |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

За́втра я бу́ду рабо́тать. (Zavtra ya budu rabotat'.) - Tomorrow I will be working / will work.

бу́ду on its own is also the future of *to be*: За́втра я бу́ду до́ма. - Tomorrow I will be at home.

### Aspect: process vs result

Almost every Russian verb comes in a pair. The **imperfective** member looks at an action as a process, a habit, or just a fact ("reading happened"). The **perfective** member looks at it as a single, completed event with a result ("the book got read").

![Timeline comparing an imperfective action shown as an ongoing band with a perfective action shown as a single completed point with a result](study/russian-zero-to-conversational/aspect-timeline.svg "Imperfective films the process; perfective takes a photo of the result")

Perfectives are usually formed with a prefix (чита́ть > **про**чита́ть), sometimes by changing the suffix (реша́ть > реш**и́**ть), and a few are different words (говори́ть > сказа́ть). You learn them as pairs:

| Imperfective | Perfective | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| де́лать | сде́лать | to do, make |
| чита́ть | прочита́ть | to read |
| писа́ть | написа́ть | to write |
| смотре́ть | посмотре́ть | to watch, look |
| звони́ть | позвони́ть | to call |
| гото́вить | пригото́вить | to cook |
| пить | вы́пить | to drink |
| есть | съесть | to eat |
| покупа́ть | купи́ть | to buy |
| реша́ть | реши́ть | to decide, solve |
| начина́ть | нача́ть | to begin |
| понима́ть | поня́ть | to understand |
| дава́ть | дать | to give |
| помога́ть | помо́чь | to help |
| говори́ть | сказа́ть | to say, tell |
| брать | взять | to take |

### How the tenses line up

The perfective has **no present tense**. Conjugating a perfective verb with present endings gives you its **future**.

| | Imperfective (process, repeated) | Perfective (one completed result) |
|---|---|---|
| Past | Я чита́л. - I was reading / used to read / did some reading. | Я прочита́л. - I read it (to the end). |
| Present | Я чита́ю. - I read / am reading. | none |
| Future | Я бу́ду чита́ть. - I will be reading. | Я прочита́ю. - I will read it (and finish). |

*Я бу́ду прочита́ть* is impossible. бу́ду only goes with imperfective infinitives.

### Choosing: four questions

1. **Is it repeated or habitual?** Imperfective. Ка́ждый день я звони́л ма́ме. - I called Mom every day. Words like ка́ждый день, всегда́ (always), ча́сто (often), обы́чно (usually) signal imperfective.
2. **Is the focus on duration or process?** Imperfective. Вчера́ я два часа́ чита́л. - Yesterday I read for two hours.
3. **Is it one action with a result, or one step in a sequence?** Perfective. Я прочита́л кни́гу. - I finished the book. Я пришёл домо́й, поу́жинал и лёг спать. - I came home, had dinner and went to bed.
4. **Are you just asking whether something ever happened?** Imperfective. Ты чита́л "Ма́стера и Маргари́ту"? - Have you (ever) read *The Master and Margarita*?

Two actions at the same time are both imperfective: Я у́жинал и смотре́л телеви́зор. - I was having dinner and watching TV. A background process interrupted by an event mixes them: Когда́ я чита́л, позвони́л друг. - While I was reading, a friend called.

### Imperatives

Build the command from the *they* form of the present (or perfective future): drop the ending; after a vowel add **-й**, after a consonant add **-и** (or **-ь** when the stress is on the stem). Add **-те** for вы.

| Infinitive | они́ form | ты | вы |
|---|---|---|---|
| чита́ть | чита́ют | чита́й | чита́йте |
| говори́ть | говоря́т | говори́ | говори́те |
| сказа́ть | ска́жут | скажи́ | скажи́те |
| позвони́ть | позвоня́т | позвони́ | позвони́те |
| помо́чь | помо́гут | помоги́ | помоги́те |
| дать (irregular) | даду́т | дай | да́йте |

Use the perfective for a one-off request (Скажи́те, пожа́луйста, где метро́? - Tell me, please, where the subway is?) and the imperfective for invitations and general instructions (Проходи́те, сади́тесь! - Come in, sit down!).

> **EXERCISE:** Choose the aspect. (1) Вчера́ я весь ве́чер (смотре́л / посмотре́л) сериа́л. (2) Я (писа́л / написа́л) письмо́ и пошёл на по́чту. (3) Ка́ждое у́тро она́ (пила́ / вы́пила) ко́фе. (4) За́втра я (бу́ду покупа́ть / куплю́) биле́ты. (Tomorrow I will buy the tickets.) (5) Ты уже́ (де́лал / сде́лал) дома́шнее зада́ние? (Have you already done your homework?) (6) Give both past forms (man, woman) of быть and мочь.
>
> **Answers:** (1) смотре́л - duration, "the whole evening" (2) написа́л - completed, then the next step (3) пила́ - habitual (4) куплю́ - one completed action in the future (5) сде́лал - asking about the result, signaled by уже́ (6) был / была́; мог / могла́

## Verbs of motion: going on foot and by vehicle

English "go" is one verb. Russian needs to know **how** you go (on foot or by vehicle) and **what shape** the movement has (one direction now, or back-and-forth / habitual).

![Two pairs of motion verbs: one-direction verbs with a single arrow, multi-direction verbs with a round trip and repeated trips](study/russian-zero-to-conversational/motion-verbs.svg "One direction right now, or round trips and habits: pick the verb by the shape of the journey")

| | One direction, in progress | Round trip, habit, aimless |
|---|---|---|
| On foot | **идти́**: иду́, идёшь, иду́т; past шёл, шла, шли | **ходи́ть**: хожу́, хо́дишь, хо́дят; past ходи́л |
| By vehicle | **е́хать**: е́ду, е́дешь, е́дут; past е́хал | **е́здить**: е́зжу, е́здишь, е́здят; past е́здил |

- Сейча́с я иду́ в магази́н. - I am walking to the store right now. (one direction, now)
- Я ча́сто хожу́ в магази́н. - I often go to the store. (habitual round trips)
- Вчера́ я ходи́л в кино́. - Yesterday I went to the cinema (and came back). The past of ходи́ть means a completed round trip.
- Когда́ я шёл в кино́, я встре́тил дру́га. - When I was on my way to the cinema, I met a friend.
- Ле́том мы е́здили в Петербу́рг. - In summer we went (made a trip) to St. Petersburg.
- Ребёнок уже́ хо́дит. - The child can already walk. (ability, aimless motion)

In a city, anything longer than a walk uses е́хать / е́здить, including the subway: Я е́ду на метро́. (by subway) Я е́зжу на рабо́ту на авто́бусе. (I commute to work by bus.) The vehicle takes **на + prepositional**.

### Going in the future: пойти́ and пое́хать

To say you *will go* or are *setting off*, use the perfective with **по-**: пойти́ (пойду́, пойдёшь; past пошёл, пошла́) and пое́хать (пое́ду, пое́дешь; past пое́хал).

- За́втра я пойду́ в музе́й. - Tomorrow I will go to the museum.
- Она́ пое́хала домо́й. - She left for home (and has not necessarily arrived).
- Пойдём! - Let's go!

For plans in the near future, the present of идти́ / е́хать works like English "I am going": Сего́дня ве́чером мы идём в теа́тр.

### Where to, where from

| Question | Place (в / на) | Person | Answer example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Где? (at) | в / на + prepositional | у + genitive | Я в шко́ле. Я у врача́. |
| Куда́? (to) | в / на + accusative | к + dative | Я иду́ в шко́лу. Я иду́ к врачу́. |
| Отку́да? (from) | из / с + genitive | от + genitive | Я иду́ из шко́лы. Я иду́ от врача́. |

в pairs with из; на pairs with с: на рабо́ту / с рабо́ты. *Home* is special: до́ма (at home), домо́й (homeward), из до́ма (from home).

### A preview of prefixes

Prefixes add direction and turn motion verbs into regular aspect pairs, which you will learn at A2-B1: при- (arrive), у- (leave), в- (go in), вы- (go out), пере- (cross), под- (approach). прийти́ / приходи́ть - to arrive; уйти́ / уходи́ть - to leave. Он пришёл в семь. - He arrived at seven. Она́ уже́ ушла́. - She has already left.

Other pairs follow the same idea: нести́ / носи́ть (to carry), лете́ть / лета́ть (to fly), бежа́ть / бе́гать (to run).

> **EXERCISE:** Choose the verb. (1) Смотри́, вон (идёт / хо́дит) наш учи́тель! (2) Ка́ждое ле́то мы (е́дем / е́здим) на мо́ре. (3) Вчера́ она́ (ходи́ла / шла) в бассе́йн. (4) За́втра я (пое́ду / е́здил) в Москву́. (5) Answer with a full sentence: Куда́ ты идёшь? (to the library) (6) Answer: Где ты был? (at a friend's place)
>
> **Answers:** (1) идёт - one direction, now (2) е́здим - repeated trips (3) ходи́ла - a completed round trip (4) пое́ду - future, setting off (5) Я иду́ в библиоте́ку. (6) Я был у дру́га. (a woman: Я была́ у дру́га.)

| 2 | два, две | 13 | трина́дцать | 50 | пятьдеся́т |

### Numbers govern case

This is the rule every learner must internalize. The **last word** of the number decides the form of the noun:

![Three bands for noun forms after numbers: 1 takes the nominative singular, 2 to 4 the genitive singular, 5 to 20 and round numbers the genitive plural](study/russian-zero-to-conversational/numbers-case.svg "After numbers: 1 = nominative, 2-4 = genitive singular, 5 and up = genitive plural")

| Number ends in | Noun form | книга | год | рубль |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (1, 21, 101) | nominative singular | одна́ кни́га | оди́н год | оди́н рубль |
| 2, 3, 4 (22, 33, 104) | genitive singular | две кни́ги | два го́да | три рубля́ |
| 5-20, 0, and 11-14 | genitive plural | пять книг | пять лет | пять рубле́й |

Note the irregular genitive plural of год (year): **лет**. That gives you ages: Мне два́дцать оди́н год. Мне два́дцать два го́да. Мне два́дцать пять лет. 11-14 always take the genitive plural, because they end in "-teen", not in 1-4: оди́ннадцать рубле́й.

### Telling time

Кото́рый час? or Ско́лько вре́мени? - What time is it? The same case rule applies to час (hour) and мину́та (minute):

- Сейча́с час. - It is one o'clock. (оди́н is dropped)
- Два часа́. Пять часо́в. - Two o'clock. Five o'clock.
- Пять часо́в два́дцать мину́т. - 5:20.
- At a time: **в** + time: в семь часо́в (at seven), в во́семь три́дцать (at 8:30).

In everyday speech people often just say the digits, like a digital clock: в семь три́дцать. The traditional way counts toward the *next* hour: полови́на восьмо́го (7:30, literally "half of the eighth"), в полови́не восьмо́го (at 7:30); без пятна́дцати во́семь (7:45, "eight minus fifteen"). Learn to understand it; you can speak digitally.

## Culture notes and common mistakes

### Culture notes that affect how you speak

- **ты / вы is a relationship, not just grammar.** Switching to ты too early can sound rude or overly familiar; staying on вы for months with a friend sounds cold. Follow the other person's lead, and use the name plus patronymic with вы in formal settings.
- **"Как дела́?" is a real question.** It is not a passing "how's it going". Expect a short honest answer, and do not ask it of a shop assistant.
- **Smiling at strangers is less common.** A neutral face in public is not unfriendly. Warmth shows up in private, and hospitality at home is generous: expect food, tea, and to be offered house slippers (та́почки) after you take your shoes off at the door.
- **Flowers come in odd numbers.** Even numbers of flowers are for funerals.
- **Superstitions you will meet:** do not shake hands or pass things across a threshold; sit down for a moment before a long trip (присе́сть на доро́жку); do not whistle indoors ("you will whistle your money away").
- **Directness.** Russian requests are often phrased as plain imperatives plus пожа́луйста, and that is polite. Overly indirect English-style hedging translated word for word can sound strange.
- **The language is wider than one country.** Russian is spoken natively or as a common language across many countries and large communities abroad, with small differences in vocabulary and accent. Standard Russian, as taught here, is understood everywhere.

### Mistakes English speakers make (and the fix)

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| Я есть студе́нт. | No "to be" in the present | Я студе́нт. |
| Я име́ю брат. | иметь is bookish for people and things you own | У меня́ есть брат. |
| Я нра́вится му́зыка. | With нра́виться, the liked thing is the subject; the person goes in the dative | Мне нра́вится му́зыка. |
| пять кни́ги | Case after numbers | пять книг |
| Я бу́ду прочита́ть. | бу́ду never takes a perfective | Я прочита́ю. or Я бу́ду чита́ть. |
| Я рабо́таю в рабо́те. | работа takes на | Я на рабо́те. |
| Я был (said by a woman) | Past tense agrees with the speaker's gender | Я была́. |
| кни́гы, ма́льчикы | Spelling rule 1 | кни́ги, ма́льчики |
| Како́е твоё и́мя? | Grammatical, but not idiomatic | Как тебя́ зову́т? |
| Ско́лько ты ста́рый? | Age uses the dative | Ско́лько тебе́ лет? |
| Я хорошо́. (as "I'm fine") | Sounds like "I am good at something" | У меня́ всё хорошо́. or Норма́льно. |

## A 16-week study plan

This plan assumes **8-10 hours a week** (about 75 minutes a day, six days a week), which adds up to roughly 130-160 hours: a solid A1 and well into A2. If you have less time, stretch each week to ten days rather than skipping steps.

### The daily routine

| Block | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Flashcards | 15 | Anki reviews first, then 10-15 new cards |
| New material | 25 | One lesson from a course or one section of this module |
| Listening | 20 | Graded podcast or learner video; relisten to yesterday's too |
| Speaking | 10 | Shadow a short clip, or describe your day aloud |
| Writing | 5 | Three sentences using today's grammar, typed in Cyrillic |

From week 6, replace one day a week with a **30-60 minute conversation** with a tutor or a language-exchange partner. Nothing else replaces the pressure of having to produce Russian in real time.

### Week by week

| Week | Focus | By the end you can |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Alphabet groups 1-3, keyboard setup, international words | Read and type any word slowly |
| 2 | Remaining letters, stress, reduction, soft consonants, italics | Read a short text aloud with marked stress |
| 3 | Greetings, survival phrases, pronouns, no "to be", gender, ты / вы | Introduce yourself and ask someone's name |
| 4 | Present tense (both conjugations), questions, negation, numbers 0-20 | Say what you do every day |
| 5 | Prepositional case, где, в / на, numbers to 100 | Say where you live, work and study |
| 6 | Accusative case, куда́, food vocabulary; first tutor session | Say what you eat, read and watch |
| 7 | Adjectives and possessives in the nominative, э́тот, family | Describe your family and home |
| 8 | Genitive singular, у меня́ есть / нет, numbers and case, money | Shop at a market and ask prices |
| 9 | Past tense, times, days of the week | Tell what you did yesterday |
| 10 | Dative: нра́виться, age, звони́ть, ну́жно; pronouns in all cases | Talk about likes, ages and needs |
| 11 | Instrumental: с, professions, занима́ться; hobbies | Talk about your job, studies and hobbies |
| 12 | Aspect pairs, the two futures; adjective endings in all cases | Plan next week and explain what you finished |
| 13 | Motion verbs, пойти́ / пое́хать, directions, transport | Ask for and give directions; describe a trip |
| 14 | Plural cases, genitive plural, months, dates | Make an appointment and talk about birthdays |
| 15 | Imperatives, short forms, comparisons; cafe and school vocabulary | Order a meal, compare two things |
| 16 | Review week: redo every exercise, record a 3-minute monologue | Hold a 5-minute conversation about yourself |

### After week 16: the road to B1

Months 5-9 are about volume, not new tables. Add genitive and instrumental plural drills, prefixed verbs of motion, participles for reading, and conditional *бы* (Я бы пошёл - I would go). Read graded readers, watch a series with Russian subtitles, keep a weekly conversation slot, and write a short journal entry every few days for a tutor to correct. If you want a certificate, the TORFL exam (ТРКИ) has elementary (A1), basic (A2) and first-certificate (B1) levels.

### Spaced repetition done right

- **Keep new cards to 10-15 a day.** Every new card creates about ten reviews over the next month. Too many new cards is the number one reason people quit.
- **Make sentence cards.** Front: an English sentence or a picture. Back: the Russian sentence with stress marks and audio. Add a second card type for Russian-to-English recognition.
- **Include grammar in the card.** For nouns: gender, genitive singular, and anything irregular (друг, pl. друзья́). For verbs: aspect partner and the я / ты forms (писа́ть: пишу́, пи́шешь; pf. написа́ть).
- **Review before new material,** every day, even if only for five minutes. A missed day costs you two.
- **Delete or rewrite leeches:** cards you keep failing usually need a better example sentence, not more repetitions.

> **EXERCISE:** Build your first 30 Anki cards from the survival phrases and the family table in this module: sentence on one side, English on the other, stress marked, audio from Forvo or a text-to-speech voice. Review them for seven days in a row and note your retention.
>
> **Answers:** A healthy deck shows 80-90% correct reviews after a week. Below 70%, your cards are too hard or too many: reduce new cards per day and shorten the sentences. Above 95%, you can raise new cards to 15-20 a day.

## Cheat sheet

**Alphabet traps:** В = v, Е = ye, Н = n, Р = r, С = s, У = oo, Х = kh. Ы = a back "i"; Ь softens; Ъ separates.

**Pronunciation:** unstressed о > [a], unstressed е / я > [i]; final б в г д ж з devoice; я е и ё ю mark a soft consonant; ж ш ц always hard, ч щ й always soft; что = [shto], -ого = [-ava].

**Spelling rules:** after г к х ж ш щ ч write и (not ы); after г к х ж ш щ ч ц write а у (not я ю); after ж ш щ ч ц unstressed о becomes е.

**Gender:** consonant / -й = masculine; -а / -я = feminine; -о / -е = neuter; -ь = learn it (-ость is feminine).

**Present tense:**

| | 1st (чита́ть) | 2nd (говори́ть) |
|---|---|---|
| я / ты / он | -ю, -ешь, -ет | -ю, -ишь, -ит |
| мы / вы / они́ | -ем, -ете, -ют | -им, -ите, -ят |

**Past:** stem + -л / -ла / -ло / -ли by gender and number. **Future:** бу́ду + imperfective infinitive, or conjugated perfective.

**Aspect:** imperfective = process, habit, "did it ever happen"; perfective = one completed result, sequence. No perfective present; no бу́ду + perfective.

**Cases (singular noun endings, masc / neut / fem -а / fem -ь):**

| Case | Questions | Endings | Triggers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nom | кто? что? | - / -о / -а / -ь | subject |
| Acc | кого́? что? куда́? | - or -а (animate) / -о / -у / -ь | object; в / на + destination; в + day |
| Gen | кого́? чего́? отку́да? | -а / -а / -ы / -и | of; нет; numbers 2+; у, из, с, от, до, без, для |
| Dat | кому́? чему́? | -у / -у / -е / -и | to/for someone; нра́виться; age; к, по |
| Inst | кем? чем? | -ом / -ом / -ой / -ью | with (с); tool; профессия; занима́ться |
| Prep | где? о чём? | -е / -е / -е / -и | в / на + location; о |

**Plural oblique cases:** dat -ам / -ям, inst -ами / -ями, prep -ах / -ях for every gender.

**Adjectives:** -ый / -ая / -ое / -ые; gen -ого, dat -ому, inst -ым, prep -ом; feminine oblique cases all -ой.

**Numbers:** 1 + nom sg; 2-4 + gen sg; 5-20 + gen pl; the last digit decides; 11-14 always gen pl. 1 год, 2 го́да, 5 лет.

**Motion:** идти́ / е́хать = one direction now; ходи́ть / е́здить = round trips, habits; пойти́ / пое́хать = will go, set off. где? в + prep; куда́? в + acc; отку́да? из + gen.

**Survival kit:** Здра́вствуйте. Спаси́бо. Пожа́луйста. Извини́те. Я не понима́ю. Повтори́те, пожа́луйста. Как сказа́ть ... по-ру́сски? Ско́лько сто́ит? Где ...? Счёт, пожа́луйста.

## Where to go next

All of these were available and free (or with a useful free tier) at the time of writing (2026). Apps and sites change their plans and regional availability, so check each one before you commit.

- **Mezhdu nami** (mezhdunami.org) - a complete, free, web-based first-year Russian textbook from the University of Kansas, with audio, glosses and free downloadable homework. The best free structured course; follow it alongside this module.
- **OpenRussian.org** - a free dictionary with stress marks, full declension and conjugation tables, aspect partners and example sentences. Its data is open.
- **Wiktionary** (en.wiktionary.org) - declension and conjugation tables with stress for nearly every Russian word.
- **Forvo** (forvo.com) - native speakers pronouncing individual words; ideal audio for flashcards.
- **Anki** (apps.ankiweb.net) - the spaced-repetition app; free on desktop, Android and the web (the iOS app is paid).
- **Tatoeba** (tatoeba.org) - a free, community-built database of example sentences with translations, good for sentence cards.
- **Duolingo** - its free Russian course is fine for the alphabet and a daily habit, weak on explaining grammar. Use it as a supplement.
- **Easy Russian** (YouTube) - street interviews in Russian cities with Russian and English subtitles. Start around week 8.
- **Real Russian Club** (YouTube) - free lessons from a certified teacher, from absolute beginner upward, plus slow Russian videos.
- **Learn Russian with Max** (podcast, and russianwithmax.com) - slow, comprehensible episodes aimed at A2-B1 listeners. A perfect listening diet for weeks 12-16 and beyond.
- **Language exchange** - Tandem and HelloTalk both have free tiers for finding native speakers who want to practice English. Use them for text chat first, then voice.

**Next steps after this module:** work through the genitive plural and prefixed motion verbs properly, start a graded reader, watch a children's series with Russian subtitles (the animated *Маша и Медве́дь*, Masha and the Bear, is a gentle start), then move to Dragunsky's short stories *Дени́скины расска́зы* as your first real book. Keep one conversation a week on the calendar, no matter what. Уда́чи! (Udachi! - Good luck!)
