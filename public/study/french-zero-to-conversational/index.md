French is the rare language that English speakers already half-know. Roughly a third of English vocabulary came in through French after 1066, the grammar is familiar Indo-European territory, and the alphabet is the one you are reading now. What trips people up is not the grammar, it is the *sound*: a spelling system full of silent letters, vowels English does not have, and words that melt into each other. This module takes you from zero to a conversational high-school level, roughly CEFR **A2 moving into B1**: you will be able to introduce yourself, order food, ask for directions, talk about your weekend in the past tense, make plans, and follow slow, clear speech. It is organised the way a good teacher would sequence a first semester and a bit: sounds first, then the core machinery (articles, adjectives, the present tense), then time travel (future and past), then pronouns, numbers, vocabulary and real-life dialogues. Every section ends with an exercise; the answers are right there, so do the work before you peek.

![A roadmap from A0 to B1 over sixteen weeks, listing the skills unlocked at each stage](study/french-zero-to-conversational/cefr-roadmap.svg "The roadmap: from first sounds to conversational French in about sixteen weeks")

A quick key before we start. French words are shown in **bold** or plain text, followed by an English-friendly respelling in parentheses and an English gloss: **bonjour** (boh(n)-zhoor) - hello. In the respellings:

| Respelling | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| zh | the *s* in "measure" | **je** (zhuh) - I |
| ü | say "ee", then round your lips as for "oo" | **tu** (tü) - you |
| oo | as in "food" | **vous** (voo) - you |
| uh | the vague vowel in "about" | **le** (luh) - the |
| ay / eh | "ay" without the glide / "e" in "bed" | **été** (ay-tay) - summer, **mère** (mehr) - mother |
| (n) | the vowel is nasal; do **not** pronounce the n | **bon** (boh(n)) - good |
| r | the French r, made in the throat (see below) | **rue** (rü) - street |

French stress is light and falls on the **last syllable of a phrase**, not of each word, so no syllable in the respellings is capitalised. Say every syllable with roughly equal weight and let the voice rise or land at the end.

## Before you start: timeline, method, and your head start

### How long does this really take?

The US Foreign Service Institute (FSI) puts French in **Category I**, the easiest group for English speakers, alongside Spanish, Italian, Dutch and a few others. FSI's estimate for that group is **24 to 30 weeks, or 600 to 750 class hours**, to reach professional working proficiency (roughly B2/C1). That is full-time, intensive study with small classes and hours of homework a day.

You are aiming lower, and that is good news. Language schools commonly use rough figures of **180 to 250 guided hours for A2** and **350 to 400 for B1** (treat these as rules of thumb, not promises; they vary by learner and method). At **10 to 12 hours per week**, which is about an hour and a half a day, sixteen weeks gets you to a solid A2 with B1 in reach for familiar topics. At 30 minutes a day, double the timeline. There is no shortcut around hours of contact with the language; there are only ways to make each hour count.

### How to study so it sticks

1. **Input before output, but not instead of output.** Listen and read a lot of material that is *slightly* above your level. Understanding comes first; speaking grows out of it. But from week one, say things out loud every day, even alone.
2. **Spaced repetition for vocabulary.** Use Anki (free on desktop, Android and the web) or any SRS app. Make cards with **whole short sentences** and **always include the article**, so you learn gender for free: not "maison = house" but "**la maison** - the house". Add audio. Cap new cards at 10 to 20 a day; reviews snowball.
3. **Shadowing for pronunciation.** Play a short clip of a native speaker (with a transcript), pause, and repeat it copying the melody, rhythm and linking exactly. Five minutes a day does more for your accent than an hour of reading rules.
4. **Learn chunks, not words.** "**Je voudrais...**" (zhuh voo-dreh) - I would like... is one unit. "**Il y a**" (eel ee ah) - there is/are is one unit. Chunks let you speak before you can analyse.
5. **Get a human early.** A tutor or a language-exchange partner by week 4 or 5 turns passive knowledge into speech. Fifteen minutes of real conversation beats an hour of app streaks.
6. **Make mistakes on purpose.** Nobody reaches A2 without saying "je suis 16 ans" at least once. Being corrected is how the rule sticks.

> **TIP:** Change your phone's language to French for a week once you know about 300 words. You already know where every button is, so the interface becomes free vocabulary practice: **Paramètres** (settings), **Envoyer** (send), **Supprimer** (delete), **Rechercher** (search).

### Cognates: your superpower

Thousands of words are the same or nearly the same in both languages: **information**, **restaurant**, **question**, **possible**, **table**, **important**. Better still, many differences follow patterns you can apply on sight:

| English | French | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -tion | -tion | nation, information, conversation |
| -ty | -té | université (university), liberté (liberty), réalité (reality) |
| -ic, -ical | -ique | musique (music), électrique (electric), politique (political) |
| -ous | -eux / -euse | délicieux (delicious), nerveux (nervous), curieux (curious) |
| -ly | -ment | rapidement (rapidly), finalement (finally), probablement (probably) |
| -ist, -ism | -iste, -isme | artiste, touriste, tourisme |
| -ize | -iser | organiser (organize), réaliser (realize, but see below) |
| s after a vowel | ^ circumflex | forêt (forest), hôpital (hospital), fête (feast/party), côte (coast), île (isle/island) |
| initial s + consonant | é- | école (school), étudiant (student), étrange (strange), épice (spice), état (state) |

The circumflex rule is especially satisfying: that little hat often marks an **s** that French dropped centuries ago and English kept. **Pâte** is paste, **bête** is beast, **hôte** is host.

### False friends: cognates that lie

Some words look English but mean something else. Learn these as a list, because they cause real misunderstandings:

| French | Looks like | Actually means |
|---|---|---|
| **actuellement** | actually | currently, at the moment (actually = **en fait**) |
| **attendre** | attend | to wait for (to attend = **assister à**) |
| **rester** | rest | to stay (to rest = **se reposer**) |
| **librairie** | library | bookshop (library = **bibliothèque**) |
| **sensible** | sensible | sensitive (sensible = **raisonnable**) |
| **éventuellement** | eventually | possibly, if need be (eventually = **finalement**) |
| **location** | location | rental (location = **endroit**, **lieu**) |
| **journée** | journey | day, daytime (journey = **voyage**, **trajet**) |
| **raisin** | raisin | grape (raisin = **raisin sec**) |
| **déception** | deception | disappointment |
| **collège** | college | middle school, ages 11 to 15 (college = **université**, **fac**) |
| **large** | large | wide (large = **grand**) |
| **blesser** | bless | to injure (to bless = **bénir**) |
| **préservatif** | preservative | condom (preservative = **conservateur**); the classic food-label blunder |
| **réaliser** | realize | to carry out, to make happen (to realize = **se rendre compte**) |

> **EXERCISE:** Translate into English: (1) Je reste à la bibliothèque. (2) Actuellement, j'attends le bus. (3) C'est une librairie. (4) Il est très sensible. (5) Elle est au collège.
>
> **Answers:** (1) I'm staying at the library. (2) At the moment, I'm waiting for the bus. (3) It's a bookshop. (4) He is very sensitive. (5) She is in middle school.

## Pronunciation: sounds, spelling, and linking

French spelling looks chaotic, but it is far more *predictable* than English: once you know the rules, you can pronounce almost any word you read. The reverse (spelling a word you hear) is harder, because many letters are silent. Get the sounds right in the first two weeks and everything else gets easier, because you will hear the grammar that is otherwise invisible.

### Vowels: the ones English does not have

French vowels are **pure**: they do not glide. English "day" slides from "eh" to "ee"; French **été** (ay-tay) holds one steady sound. Keep your jaw and lips still while the vowel lasts.

![A vowel chart plotting French oral vowels by tongue position with example words, next to the four nasal vowels and their spellings](study/french-zero-to-conversational/vowel-map.svg "French vowels: rounded front vowels and nasal vowels are the new territory for English speakers")

The most important contrast for beginners is **u** versus **ou**:

| Spelling | IPA | How | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **i** | [i] | "ee", lips spread | **si** (see) - if, **lit** (lee) - bed |
| **u** | [y] | tongue in "ee" position, lips rounded tight | **tu** (tü) - you, **rue** (rü) - street, **lu** (lü) - read |
| **ou** | [u] | "oo" as in food | **tout** (too) - all, **roue** (roo) - wheel, **loup** (loo) - wolf |

Minimal pairs that change meaning: **dessus** (duh-sü) - on top / **dessous** (duh-soo) - underneath; **vu** (vü) - seen / **vous** (voo) - you; **rue** - street / **roue** - wheel. Practise the trick: say "ee", freeze your tongue, then push your lips forward into a tight circle. That is **u**.

The **e** family:

| Spelling | IPA | Sound | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **é**, final **-er**, **-ez** | [e] | "ay" without a glide | **été** - summer, **parler** (par-lay) - to speak, **nez** (nay) - nose |
| **è**, **ê**, **ai**, **e** + two consonants | [ɛ] | "e" in bed | **mère** - mother, **fête** - party, **lait** (leh) - milk, **belle** (bel) - beautiful |
| **e** at end of syllable | [ə] or silent | "uh", often dropped | **le** (luh), **petit** (puh-tee, often "ptee"), **samedi** (sam-dee) |
| **eu**, **œu** | [ø] / [œ] | "uh" with rounded lips | **deux** (duh) - two, **peur** (puhr) - fear, **sœur** (suhr) - sister |

Other spellings that always make the same sound: **au** and **eau** = "oh" (**beau** (boh) - beautiful, **eau** (oh) - water); **oi** = "wah" (**moi** (mwah) - me, **trois** (trwah) - three); **ch** = "sh" (**chat** (shah) - cat); **gn** = "ny" as in canyon (**montagne** (moh(n)-tahny) - mountain); **qu** = "k" (**qui** (kee) - who); **ill** after a vowel usually = "y" (**fille** (fee-y) - girl, **travail** (trah-vahy) - work; but **ville** (veel) - town and **mille** (meel) - thousand are exceptions).

### Nasal vowels

When a vowel is followed by **n** or **m** that ends the syllable (end of word, or before another consonant), the vowel becomes **nasal**: air goes through your nose as well as your mouth, and the n/m itself is **not pronounced**. There are three (or four) nasal vowels:

| Spellings | IPA | Respelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **an, am, en, em** | [ɑ̃] | ah(n) | **enfant** (ah(n)-fah(n)) - child, **temps** (tah(n)) - time, **chambre** (shah(n)br) - bedroom |
| **on, om** | [ɔ̃] | oh(n) | **bon** (boh(n)) - good, **nom** (noh(n)) - name, **onze** (oh(n)z) - eleven |
| **in, im, ain, ein, (y)en** | [ɛ̃] | eh(n) | **vin** (veh(n)) - wine, **pain** (peh(n)) - bread, **plein** (pleh(n)) - full, **bien** (byeh(n)) - well |
| **un, um** | [œ̃] | uh(n) | **un** (uh(n)) - one, **parfum** (par-fuh(n)) - perfume |

In most of France today, **un** and **in** have merged into the same sound, so do not lose sleep over the fourth one. The distinction you must make is **an** versus **on**: **vent** (vah(n)) - wind versus **vont** (voh(n)) - they go; **sans** - without versus **son** - his/her/sound.

The key rule is what *breaks* nasality: if the n/m is followed by a vowel, or is doubled, the vowel is **oral** and the n is pronounced. **bon** (boh(n)) - good, masculine, versus **bonne** (bun) - good, feminine; **un** (uh(n)) versus **une** (ün); **Américain** (ah-may-ree-keh(n)) versus **Américaine** (ah-may-ree-ken). This is how you *hear* gender in many adjectives.

### The French r

The French **r** is made at the back of the throat, close to where you make "g" and "k", with a light friction, like a very soft gargle. Say "ahh", then lift the back of your tongue towards the soft palate until you hear a faint rasp. Practise with **rouge** - red, **Paris** (pah-ree), **très** (treh) - very, **merci** (mehr-see) - thank you. Tip: start from the word **gra-** as in "grab" and soften the g into friction. Do not roll it like Spanish and do not use the English r; both mark you as foreign instantly, although you will still be understood.

### Silent letters

**Final consonants are usually silent.** **petit** (puh-tee), **grand** (grah(n)), **trop** (troh) - too much, **les** (leh), **ils parlent** (eel parl). The verb ending **-ent** in the plural is completely silent, which means **il parle** and **ils parlent** sound identical.

The classic exception mnemonic is **CaReFuL**: final **c, r, f, l** are often pronounced. **parc** (park), **sac** (sak) - bag, **mer** (mehr) - sea, **chef** (shef), **neuf** (nuhf) - nine/new, **sel** (sel) - salt, **avril** (ah-vreel). But the **-er** infinitive ending is always "ay" (**parler**, **manger**), and there are exceptions (**tabac**, **clef**), so check new words by listening.

**H is always silent.** **hôtel** (oh-tel), **homme** (um) - man, **heure** (uhr) - hour. There are two kinds of h, though. Most are **h muet** ("mute h"): the word behaves as if it starts with a vowel (**l'homme**, **les‿hommes** with a z sound). A minority, mostly of Germanic origin, are **h aspiré** ("aspirated h", though it is still silent): they block elision and liaison (**le héros** - the hero, **les / haricots** - the beans, **la honte** - shame). Dictionaries mark them with an asterisk or apostrophe.

### Liaison, elision and enchaînement

French hates the gap between two vowel sounds and loves a smooth flow. Three mechanisms make words run together, and they are why spoken French sounds like one long word to beginners.

![Three panels showing elision, liaison and enchaînement with examples, plus lists of required and forbidden liaisons](study/french-zero-to-conversational/linking.svg "Linking: elision drops a vowel, liaison wakes up a silent consonant, enchaînement slides a spoken one forward")

**Elision** is written: a short word drops its final vowel before a vowel or mute h and takes an apostrophe. It is **mandatory**.

| Full form | Elided | Example |
|---|---|---|
| le, la | l' | **l'ami** - the friend, **l'eau** - the water, **l'hôtel** |
| je | j' | **j'aime** - I like, **j'habite** - I live |
| me, te, se | m', t', s' | **je m'appelle** - my name is, **il s'amuse** - he's having fun |
| ne | n' | **je n'ai pas** - I don't have |
| de | d' | **d'accord** - OK, **beaucoup d'eau** - a lot of water |
| que | qu' | **qu'est-ce que...** - what... |
| si | s' (only before il/ils) | **s'il vous plaît** - please; but **si elle** stays |

**Liaison** is a normally silent final consonant that gets pronounced *because the next word starts with a vowel sound*, and it attaches to that next word. Consonants change on the way: **s**, **x** and **z** sound like **z**; **d** sounds like **t**; **n** is pronounced after the nasal vowel.

- **les‿amis** (leh-zah-mee) - the friends
- **deux‿enfants** (duh-zah(n)-fah(n)) - two children
- **un grand‿homme** (uh(n) grah(n)-tum) - a great man
- **nous‿avons** (noo-zah-voh(n)) - we have
- **un‿ami** (uh(n)-nah-mee) - a friend

Liaison is **required** after determiners (les, des, un, mon, ces, deux...), between a subject pronoun and its verb (nous‿avons, ils‿ont, on‿a), between an adjective and the noun it precedes (petit‿ami, bon‿anniversaire), after short prepositions and adverbs (en‿Italie, chez‿eux, très‿important) and in set phrases (tout‿à fait, c'est-à-dire (seh-tah-deer), de temps‿en temps). It is **forbidden** after **et** (**toi et / elle**), before h aspiré (**les / héros**), after a singular noun (**un étudiant / anglais**) and after names. Everywhere else it is optional, and the more formal the speech, the more liaisons you hear.

> **WARNING:** **Ils ont** (eel-zoh(n)) - they have and **ils sont** (eel-soh(n)) - they are differ only by liaison: a **z** sound versus an **s** sound. Get this wrong and "they have a dog" becomes "they are a dog". Say both ten times.

**Enchaînement** ("chaining") is liaison's quieter sibling: a consonant that is *already* pronounced slides over to start the next word. **il‿arrive** (ee-lah-reev) - he's arriving, **une‿amie** (ü-nah-mee) - a friend, **avec‿elle** (ah-veh-kel) - with her. You do not have to learn rules for it; just never pause between words inside a phrase.

### Accents: what they actually do

French accents are not stress marks. They either change the sound, distinguish two words, or are historical traces.

| Accent | On | Effect | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **aigu** (acute) ´ | é only | makes "ay" | **café**, **été**, **étudiant** |
| **grave** ` | è, à, ù | on è, makes "eh"; on à/ù, only distinguishes words | **mère**; **a** (has) vs **à** (to/at); **ou** (or) vs **où** (where) |
| **circonflexe** ^ | â ê î ô û | often a lost s; ê = "eh", ô = closed "oh" | **forêt**, **hôpital**, **sûr** (sure) vs **sur** (on) |
| **tréma** ¨ | ë ï ü | pronounce the two vowels separately | **Noël** (no-el), **naïf** (nah-eef) |
| **cédille** ¸ | ç | makes c soft ("s") before a, o, u | **ça** (sah) - that, **garçon** (gar-soh(n)) - boy, **français** |

> **NOTE:** The 1990 spelling reform made the circumflex optional on **i** and **u** where it changes nothing (**connaître** or **connaitre**, **coût** or **cout**), and many French schools teach the reformed spellings. You will see both in 2026. The circumflex stays where it distinguishes words: **sûr / sur**, **dû / du**, **mûr / mur**.

> **EXERCISE:** (a) Which final consonants are pronounced? **parc, grand, sel, chanter, chef, ils mangent, sac, trop.** (b) Mark the required liaisons: **les amis, nous avons, un grand homme, toi et elle, les héros.**
>
> **Answers:** (a) Pronounced: park (c), sel (l), chef (f), sac (c). Silent: grand, chanter (the r; it ends in "ay"), mangent (-ent), trop. (b) les‿amis (z), nous‿avons (z), grand‿homme (t). No liaison after **et**, and none before h aspiré in **les / héros**.

**Enchanté** takes an extra **e** in writing when a woman says it (**enchantée**); the pronunciation does not change. You will see this pattern everywhere.

### Tu or vous?

French has two words for "you" in the singular, and choosing wrong is a social error, not a grammar one.

- **tu** - you (informal, one person): family, friends, children, pets, classmates, people your age in casual settings, and anyone who says tu to you first in a relaxed context.
- **vous** - you (formal singular, *and* every plural): strangers, shop staff, teachers, older people, anyone in a professional setting, and any group of two or more people regardless of how well you know them.

When in doubt, use **vous**. Being too polite is a small misstep; being too familiar can be read as rude. The switch to tu is often explicit: **On se tutoie ?** (ohn suh tü-twah) - shall we use tu? The verbs are **tutoyer** (to use tu) and **vouvoyer** (to use vous).

> **NOTE:** Norms vary by place and age. In Quebec, tu is far more common, even with strangers. Among French students and young colleagues, tu is the default. In a Paris shop or with your friend's grandparents, it is vous.

### Culture: the magic word "bonjour"

In France, **bonjour** is not optional small talk; it is the key that opens every interaction. Walking into a shop, a bakery or a doctor's waiting room, you say **Bonjour** (ideally **Bonjour madame** or **Bonjour monsieur**) before anything else. Asking a question without it ("Where's the station?") comes across as rude, and the difference in how you are treated is dramatic. Leaving, you say **Merci, au revoir** or **Bonne journée**. Friends and family greet each other with **la bise**, a light cheek-to-cheek air kiss; the number of kisses (one to four) depends on the region, so let the other person lead.

> **EXERCISE:** Tu or vous? (1) Your host family's 10-year-old. (2) A pharmacist. (3) Two classmates. (4) Your French teacher. (5) Your partner's grandmother, the first time you meet.
>
> **Answers:** (1) tu. (2) vous. (3) vous (plural, always). (4) vous (unless the teacher explicitly invites tu, which some do with adults). (5) vous.

## Nouns, articles and adjectives

### Every noun has a gender

Every French noun is **masculine** or **feminine**, including objects and ideas: **le livre** - the book is masculine, **la table** - the table is feminine. Gender is mostly arbitrary, so the only reliable method is to learn every noun **with its article**. Some endings give you strong hints:

| Usually masculine | Usually feminine |
|---|---|
| -age: **le fromage**, **le voyage** (exceptions: **la page**, **la plage**, **l'image**) | -tion, -sion: **la nation**, **la télévision** |
| -ment: **le moment**, **le gouvernement** | -té: **la liberté**, **la santé** (exceptions: **l'été**, **le côté**) |
| -eau: **le bureau**, **le gâteau** (exceptions: **l'eau**, **la peau**) | -ure, -ette, -ence: **la voiture**, **la baguette**, **la différence** |
| -isme, -oir, -ier: **le tourisme**, **le soir**, **le cahier** | -ie, -ée: **la boulangerie**, **l'idée** (exceptions: **le musée**, **le lycée**) |

Plurals usually add **-s**, which is silent: **le livre / les livres** sound the same except for the article. Words in **-eau** and most in **-al** change: **le gâteau / les gâteaux**, **le journal / les journaux**. Since the noun often sounds the same, **the article carries the number** in speech. This is why articles matter so much in French.

### Articles: definite, indefinite, partitive

| | Masculine | Feminine | Before vowel/mute h | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Definite (the) | **le** | **la** | **l'** | **les** |
| Indefinite (a, some) | **un** | **une** | un/une | **des** |
| Partitive (some, any) | **du** | **de la** | **de l'** | **des** |

French almost never leaves a noun bare, where English happily does: "I like coffee", "I want bread", "there are cars". Each of those needs an article in French, and choosing which one is the real skill.

![Decision flow for choosing le, un, du or de before a French noun](study/french-zero-to-conversational/article-choice.svg "Which article? Ask what you mean: the thing in general, one countable item, or an unmeasured amount")

- **Definite** for specific things *and* for things in general, including after verbs of liking: **J'aime le café.** - I like coffee (in general). **Les chats sont indépendants.** - Cats are independent.
- **Indefinite** for one countable thing, or several: **J'ai un frère et des cousins.** - I have a brother and (some) cousins.
- **Partitive** for an unmeasured amount of something uncountable: **Je bois du café.** - I drink (some) coffee. **Tu veux de l'eau ?** - Do you want (some) water? **Il faut de la patience.** - It takes patience.

Compare **J'aime le fromage** (I like cheese, all cheese as a concept) with **Je mange du fromage** (I eat some cheese, a portion of it). Two traps:

1. **After a negation, un/une/des/du/de la become de (d')**: **J'ai un chien. → Je n'ai pas de chien.** **Je bois du lait. → Je ne bois pas de lait.** Exception: with **être**, the article stays: **Ce n'est pas un chien, c'est un loup.**
2. **After expressions of quantity, only de**: **beaucoup de sucre** - a lot of sugar, **un kilo de tomates**, **une bouteille d'eau**, **trop de travail** - too much work.

Also watch **à** and **de** merging with **le** and **les**: **à + le = au**, **à + les = aux**, **de + le = du**, **de + les = des**. So **Je vais au cinéma** (to the cinema), **le livre du professeur** (the teacher's book). With **la** and **l'** nothing merges: **à la plage**, **de l'école**.

### Possessives and demonstratives

The possessive agrees with the **thing owned**, not with the owner. **son**, **sa** and **ses** all mean both "his" and "her".

| | Masc. sing. | Fem. sing. | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| my | **mon** | **ma** | **mes** |
| your (tu) | **ton** | **ta** | **tes** |
| his / her / its | **son** | **sa** | **ses** |
| our | **notre** | **notre** | **nos** |
| your (vous) | **votre** | **votre** | **vos** |
| their | **leur** | **leur** | **leurs** |

**Sa voiture** is his car or her car; the context tells you whose. Before a feminine noun starting with a vowel, use the masculine form for sound: **mon amie** (moh(n)-nah-mee), never "ma amie". Demonstratives work the same way: **ce livre** - this/that book, **cet ami** (before a vowel), **cette maison**, **ces gens**. Add **-ci** or **-là** to point: **ce livre-ci** - this book here, **ce livre-là** - that book there.

### Adjective agreement

Adjectives agree in gender and number with their noun. The default is: add **-e** for feminine, **-s** for plural.

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Masculine | un ami **grand** | des amis **grands** |
| Feminine | une amie **grande** | des amies **grandes** |

Agreement is often audible: **grand** (grah(n)) versus **grande** (grah(n)d). The added **e** wakes up the silent consonant. Common patterns:

| Masculine | Feminine | Example |
|---|---|---|
| -e | no change | **jeune**, **rouge**, **facile** |
| -eux | -euse | **heureux / heureuse** - happy |
| -if | -ive | **sportif / sportive** |
| -er | -ère | **cher / chère** - expensive, dear |
| -on, -en, -el | -onne, -enne, -elle | **bon / bonne**, **italien / italienne**, **cruel / cruelle** |
| -c | -che / -que | **blanc / blanche** - white, **public / publique** |

Three very common irregulars have a special form before a masculine noun starting with a vowel sound:

| Masc. | Masc. before vowel | Fem. | Masc. plural | Fem. plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **beau** | **bel** (un bel homme) | **belle** | **beaux** | **belles** |
| **nouveau** | **nouvel** (un nouvel ami) | **nouvelle** | **nouveaux** | **nouvelles** |
| **vieux** | **vieil** (un vieil hôtel) | **vieille** | **vieux** | **vieilles** |

### Placement: after, except BAGS

Most French adjectives come **after** the noun: **une voiture rouge** - a red car, **un film intéressant**, **la cuisine française**. Colours, nationalities, shapes and long adjectives always follow. A short list of very common adjectives goes **before**, remembered as **BAGS**:

- **B**eauty: **beau**, **joli** - pretty
- **A**ge: **jeune** - young, **vieux**, **nouveau**
- **G**oodness: **bon**, **mauvais** - bad, **gentil** - kind
- **S**ize: **grand** - big/tall, **petit**, **gros** - fat/big, **long**

**une jolie maison blanche** - a pretty white house; **un petit chat noir** - a small black cat. A handful of adjectives change meaning with position: **mon ancien professeur** - my former teacher versus **une maison ancienne** - an old house; **un cher ami** - a dear friend versus **un livre cher** - an expensive book; **ma propre chambre** - my own room versus **une chambre propre** - a clean room.

> **EXERCISE:** Agree and place the adjective. (1) une maison (beau). (2) un hôtel (vieux). (3) des chemises (blanc). (4) une femme (heureux). (5) des livres (intéressant). (6) Fill in the article: J'aime ___ chocolat, mais je ne mange pas ___ chocolat le soir. Je bois ___ eau et je mange ___ pomme.
>
> **Answers:** (1) une belle maison. (2) un vieil hôtel. (3) des chemises blanches. (4) une femme heureuse. (5) des livres intéressants. (6) J'aime **le** chocolat, mais je ne mange pas **de** chocolat le soir. Je bois **de l'**eau et je mange **une** pomme.

## The present tense

### Subject pronouns

| French | English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **je** (j') | I | elides before a vowel: **j'aime** |
| **tu** | you (informal singular) | |
| **il / elle / on** | he, it / she, it / one, we | **on** takes the same verb form as il |
| **nous** | we | formal and written; speech prefers **on** |
| **vous** | you (formal or plural) | |
| **ils / elles** | they | **ils** for any group with at least one masculine member |

French has no "it" pronoun separate from he and she: a table is **elle**, a book is **il**. And French has only one present tense: **je mange** means I eat, I am eating, and I do eat.

### Regular verbs: -er, -ir, -re

About 90% of French verbs end in **-er**, and almost all of them are regular. Drop the ending and add the endings to the stem.

| | **parler** - to speak | **finir** - to finish | **vendre** - to sell |
|---|---|---|---|
| je | parl**e** | fin**is** | vend**s** |
| tu | parl**es** | fin**is** | vend**s** |
| il/elle/on | parl**e** | fin**it** | vend |
| nous | parl**ons** | fin**issons** | vend**ons** |
| vous | parl**ez** | fin**issez** | vend**ez** |
| ils/elles | parl**ent** | fin**issent** | vend**ent** |

The great news hides in the pronunciation: for -er verbs, **parle, parles, parle, parlent** all sound identical (parl). You really have only three spoken forms: parl, parlons (par-loh(n)), parlez (par-lay). The written forms matter for writing; the speaking load is light.

Useful regular -er verbs: **aimer** (to like/love), **habiter** (to live), **travailler** (to work), **étudier** (to study), **regarder** (to watch), **écouter** (to listen to), **jouer** (to play), **chercher** (to look for), **trouver** (to find), **donner** (to give), **penser** (to think), **arriver**, **rester**, **commencer** (to begin), **manger** (to eat), **acheter** (to buy). Some have spelling tweaks to keep the sound consistent: **nous mangeons**, **nous commençons**, **j'achète**, **j'appelle**.

Regular -ir verbs like finir: **choisir** (to choose), **réussir** (to succeed), **grandir** (to grow up). Regular -re verbs like vendre: **attendre** (to wait for), **entendre** (to hear), **répondre** (to answer), **perdre** (to lose).

### The irregular nine

These are the most frequent verbs in the language. Learn them until you can recite them in your sleep; they also build the other tenses.

| | **être** - to be | **avoir** - to have | **aller** - to go | **faire** - to do/make |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| je | suis | ai (j'ai) | vais | fais |
| tu | es | as | vas | fais |
| il/elle/on | est | a | va | fait |
| nous | sommes | avons | allons | faisons |
| vous | êtes | avez | allez | faites |
| ils/elles | sont | ont | vont | font |

| | **pouvoir** - can | **vouloir** - to want | **devoir** - must | **prendre** - to take | **venir** - to come |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| je | peux | veux | dois | prends | viens |
| tu | peux | veux | dois | prends | viens |
| il/elle/on | peut | veut | doit | prend | vient |
| nous | pouvons | voulons | devons | prenons | venons |
| vous | pouvez | voulez | devez | prenez | venez |
| ils/elles | peuvent | veulent | doivent | prennent | viennent |

Verbs built on these conjugate the same way: **comprendre** (to understand) and **apprendre** (to learn) like prendre; **revenir** (to come back) and **devenir** (to become) like venir. **Pouvoir**, **vouloir** and **devoir** are followed directly by an infinitive: **Je peux venir.** - I can come. **Tu dois travailler.** - You must work. **Il veut partir.** - He wants to leave.

A few idioms use **avoir** or **faire** where English uses "to be", and they are among the first things to get right:

| French | English |
|---|---|
| **J'ai 16 ans.** | I'm 16 (literally "I have 16 years") |
| **J'ai faim / soif / chaud / froid / peur.** | I'm hungry / thirsty / hot / cold / scared |
| **J'ai raison / tort.** | I'm right / wrong |
| **J'ai besoin de...** | I need... |
| **Il fait beau / chaud / froid.** | The weather's nice / hot / cold |
| **Il y a...** | There is / there are... |

### Reflexive verbs

Many daily-routine verbs are **reflexive**: the action loops back to the subject, marked by a pronoun that matches it. **se lever** - to get up: **je me lève, tu te lèves, il se lève, nous nous levons, vous vous levez, ils se lèvent**. Others: **se réveiller** (to wake up), **s'appeler** (to be called), **s'habiller** (to get dressed), **se coucher** (to go to bed), **s'amuser** (to have fun), **se dépêcher** (to hurry). **Je me réveille à sept heures, je me douche et je m'habille.** - I wake up at seven, shower and get dressed.

### Three idioms that punch above their weight

- **depuis + present** for something that started in the past and is still going: **J'apprends le français depuis trois mois.** - I've been learning French for three months. English uses a perfect tense here; French uses the present, because it is still true.
- **venir de + infinitive** for the recent past: **Je viens de manger.** - I've just eaten.
- **être en train de + infinitive** to stress "in the middle of": **Je suis en train de cuisiner.** - I'm (busy) cooking.

> **EXERCISE:** Conjugate: (1) nous (prendre) (2) ils (venir) (3) tu (vouloir) (4) vous (faire) (5) elles (aller) (6) je (finir) (7) on (être). Then translate: (8) I'm cold. (9) I've been living here for two years.
>
> **Answers:** (1) prenons (2) viennent (3) veux (4) faites (5) vont (6) finis (7) est (8) J'ai froid. (9) J'habite ici depuis deux ans.

## Negation and questions

### Saying no: ne... pas and friends

Negation wraps the conjugated verb in two pieces: **ne** before, **pas** after. **ne** becomes **n'** before a vowel.

- **Je parle anglais. → Je ne parle pas anglais.** - I don't speak English.
- **Il aime le sport. → Il n'aime pas le sport.**
- With a reflexive, **ne** goes before the pronoun: **Je ne me lève pas tôt.** - I don't get up early.
- With verb + infinitive, wrap the conjugated verb: **Je ne veux pas partir.** - I don't want to leave.

Swap **pas** for other words to change the meaning:

| Negation | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **ne... pas** | not | **Je ne sais pas.** - I don't know. |
| **ne... jamais** | never | **Il ne mange jamais de viande.** - He never eats meat. |
| **ne... plus** | no longer, not anymore | **Elle n'habite plus ici.** - She doesn't live here anymore. |
| **ne... rien** | nothing | **Je ne vois rien.** - I can't see anything. |
| **ne... personne** | nobody | **Il n'y a personne.** - There's nobody there. |
| **ne... que** | only (not really a negation) | **Je n'ai que dix euros.** - I only have ten euros. |

The answer to a negative question uses **si**, not **oui**: **Tu ne viens pas ? - Si, je viens !** - You're not coming? - Yes, I am!

### Asking questions: three registers

French has three ways to ask a yes/no question, from casual to formal. All three are correct; they differ in register.

| Register | Method | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Casual (speech) | Intonation: raise your voice at the end | **Tu parles anglais ?** |
| Neutral (everywhere) | Put **est-ce que** in front | **Est-ce que tu parles anglais ?** |
| Formal (writing, polite) | Invert verb and pronoun, with a hyphen | **Parles-tu anglais ?** |

**Est-ce que** (es-kuh) literally means "is it that", but just treat it as a spoken question mark. In inversion, if the verb ends in a vowel and the pronoun starts with one, insert **-t-** for sound: **Parle-t-il anglais ?** **A-t-elle un frère ?** **Va-t-on au cinéma ?** In spoken French today, inversion is mostly limited to fixed phrases (**Comment allez-vous ?**, **Quelle heure est-il ?**) and formal situations.

### Question words

| French | English | Example (neutral / casual) |
|---|---|---|
| **qui** | who | **Qui est-ce ?** - Who is it? |
| **que / quoi** | what | **Qu'est-ce que tu fais ?** / **Tu fais quoi ?** - What are you doing? |
| **où** | where | **Où est-ce que tu habites ?** / **Tu habites où ?** |
| **quand** | when | **Quand est-ce qu'il arrive ?** / **Il arrive quand ?** |
| **comment** | how | **Comment tu t'appelles ?** - What's your name? |
| **pourquoi** | why | **Pourquoi est-ce que tu ris ?** - Why are you laughing? (answer: **parce que...**) |
| **combien (de)** | how much, how many | **Combien ça coûte ?** - How much is it? **Tu as combien de frères ?** |
| **quel / quelle / quels / quelles** | which, what | **Quelle heure est-il ?** **Quel est ton film préféré ?** |

**Quel** agrees with its noun like an adjective, but all four forms sound the same (kel). Note that **que** becomes **quoi** at the end of a sentence or after a preposition: **C'est quoi ?** - What is it? (casual), **Avec quoi ?** - With what?

> **EXERCISE:** (1) Make negative: Je mange de la viande. (2) Make negative with "never": Elle boit du café. (3) Turn "Vous avez des enfants." into an est-ce que question and an inversion question. (4) Invert: Il aime le jazz. (5) Answer yes to: Tu n'as pas faim ?
>
> **Answers:** (1) Je ne mange pas de viande. (2) Elle ne boit jamais de café. (3) Est-ce que vous avez des enfants ? / Avez-vous des enfants ? (4) Aime-t-il le jazz ? (5) Si, j'ai faim !

## Talking about the future and the past

### The near future: aller + infinitive

The easiest way to talk about the future is exactly the English "going to": present of **aller** plus an infinitive. It is the everyday spoken future.

- **Je vais manger.** - I'm going to eat.
- **On va voir un film ce soir.** - We're going to see a film tonight.
- **Ils ne vont pas venir.** - They aren't going to come. (negation wraps **aller**)

### The passé composé: what happened

The **passé composé** (compound past) is the main spoken past tense. It translates both "I ate" and "I have eaten". It has two parts: an auxiliary (**avoir** or **être**) in the present, plus a **past participle**.

Regular participles: **-er → -é** (parler → **parlé**), **-ir → -i** (finir → **fini**), **-re → -u** (vendre → **vendu**). All sound the same for -er verbs as the infinitive (parler and parlé are both par-lay).

- **J'ai mangé une pizza.** - I ate a pizza / I have eaten a pizza.
- **Nous avons fini nos devoirs.** - We finished our homework.
- **Tu as attendu longtemps ?** - Did you wait long?

The most common verbs have irregular participles, and you need them from week one:

| Infinitive | Participle | Infinitive | Participle |
|---|---|---|---|
| être | **été** | voir (to see) | **vu** |
| avoir | **eu** | boire (to drink) | **bu** |
| faire | **fait** | lire (to read) | **lu** |
| prendre | **pris** | pouvoir | **pu** |
| mettre (to put) | **mis** | vouloir | **voulu** |
| dire (to say) | **dit** | devoir | **dû** |
| écrire (to write) | **écrit** | savoir (to know) | **su** |
| ouvrir (to open) | **ouvert** | recevoir (to receive) | **reçu** |

In the negative, **ne... pas** wraps the auxiliary, not the participle: **Je n'ai pas mangé.** - I didn't eat. **Il n'a rien dit.** - He said nothing.

### The house of être

About twenty verbs use **être** instead of avoir. Most describe **movement or a change of state**, and they can be pictured as things you do in and around a house: arrive, go in, go up the stairs, come down, go out, fall out of the window, be born, die. The acronym **DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP** lists them, but the house picture is easier to remember.

![A house diagram with each room showing a pair of verbs that use être in the passé composé, with their past participles](study/french-zero-to-conversational/maison-etre.svg "La maison d'être: movement and change-of-state verbs take être in the passé composé")

With être, **the participle agrees with the subject** like an adjective: add **-e** for feminine and **-s** for plural.

- **Il est allé à Paris.** - He went to Paris.
- **Elle est allée à Paris.** - She went to Paris.
- **Ils sont arrivés hier.** - They arrived yesterday.
- **Elles sont venues avec nous.** - They (fem.) came with us.
- **Je suis né(e) en 2010.** - I was born in 2010.

**All reflexive verbs** also use être: **Je me suis levé(e) à sept heures.** - I got up at seven. **Elle s'est bien amusée.** - She had a great time.

> **NOTE:** Six être verbs switch to **avoir** when they take a direct object: **monter, descendre, sortir, rentrer, retourner, passer**. **Je suis sorti.** - I went out, but **J'ai sorti la poubelle.** - I took the bin out. **Elle est montée.** - She went up, but **Elle a monté les valises.** - She carried the suitcases up. Learn this at A2; do not worry about it in week one.

There is one agreement rule with avoir, too, which you will meet in writing: the participle agrees with a **direct object that comes before the verb**, usually a pronoun. **Tu as vu les photos ? - Oui, je les ai vues.** It is rarely audible, except with participles ending in a consonant: **la lettre que j'ai écrite** (ay-kreet).

### The imparfait: what was going on

The **imparfait** describes the background: what things were like, what was in progress, and what used to happen repeatedly. Formation is the most regular thing in French: take the **nous** form of the present, drop **-ons**, add the endings **-ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient**.

| | **parler** (nous parlons) | **faire** (nous faisons) | **être** (only irregular stem: ét-) |
|---|---|---|---|
| je | parlais | faisais | étais |
| tu | parlais | faisais | étais |
| il/elle/on | parlait | faisait | était |
| nous | parlions | faisions | étions |
| vous | parliez | faisiez | étiez |
| ils/elles | parlaient | faisaient | étaient |

Five of the six forms sound the same (par-leh), so the imparfait is easy to say.

### Passé composé or imparfait?

This is the big conceptual step of A2. The best intuition is a **film**: the imparfait sets the scene and describes the ongoing situation; the passé composé moves the plot forward with completed events.

![A timeline with a continuous band for imparfait background actions and sharp markers for passé composé events that interrupt it](study/french-zero-to-conversational/pc-vs-imparfait.svg "Imparfait paints the background; passé composé marks the events that happen against it")

| Imparfait (background) | Passé composé (event) |
|---|---|
| Description: **Il faisait beau. J'étais fatigué.** | A completed action: **J'ai mangé une pizza.** |
| In progress: **Je lisais...** - I was reading... | An interruption: **...quand le téléphone a sonné.** - ...when the phone rang. |
| Habit: **Quand j'étais petit, je jouais au foot tous les samedis.** - I used to play... | A one-off: **Samedi, j'ai joué au foot.** - On Saturday I played football. |
| Signal words: **d'habitude**, **tous les jours**, **souvent**, **pendant que** | Signal words: **hier**, **soudain**, **tout à coup**, **une fois**, **samedi dernier** |

Put together, a story sounds like this: **Il pleuvait et je regardais la télé. Tout à coup, quelqu'un a frappé à la porte. C'était mon voisin. Il avait l'air inquiet.** - It was raining and I was watching TV. Suddenly, someone knocked on the door. It was my neighbour. He looked worried.

### The futur simple

The simple future is used for plans, predictions and promises, and it is more common in writing and in more formal speech. For most verbs, take the **infinitive** (drop the final **e** of -re verbs) and add **-ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont** (notice: the present of avoir).

**parler → je parlerai, tu parleras, il parlera, nous parlerons, vous parlerez, ils parleront.** **prendre → je prendrai.**

Irregular stems (the endings stay regular): **être → ser-**, **avoir → aur-**, **aller → ir-**, **faire → fer-**, **pouvoir → pourr-**, **vouloir → voudr-**, **devoir → devr-**, **venir → viendr-**, **voir → verr-**, **savoir → saur-**. **Demain, il fera beau.** - Tomorrow the weather will be nice. **Je t'appellerai ce soir.** - I'll call you tonight. **On verra.** - We'll see.

### The conditionnel: the politeness tense

The conditional uses the **future stem** with the **imparfait endings**: **je voudrais, tu voudrais, il voudrait, nous voudrions, vous voudriez, ils voudraient**. For a beginner, it is above all the tool for sounding polite:

- **Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît.** - I'd like a coffee, please. (Much softer than **je veux**, which sounds like a demand.)
- **Pourriez-vous m'aider ?** - Could you help me?
- **J'aimerais partir à 18 heures.** - I'd like to leave at 6 pm.
- **Tu devrais dormir.** - You should sleep.

And it expresses hypotheticals with **si + imparfait**: **Si j'avais le temps, je voyagerais.** - If I had the time, I would travel. Never put the conditional right after **si**.

> **EXERCISE:** (1) Passé composé: Elle (partir) hier. Nous (manger) au restaurant. Ils (arriver) à midi. Je (voir) ce film. Marie (se lever) à sept heures. (2) Imparfait or passé composé: Quand j'(être) petit, je (jouer) au foot tous les samedis. Je (lire) quand le téléphone (sonner). (3) Make polite: Je veux un croissant.
>
> **Answers:** (1) Elle est partie hier. Nous avons mangé au restaurant. Ils sont arrivés à midi. J'ai vu ce film. Marie s'est levée à sept heures. (2) Quand j'étais petit, je jouais au foot tous les samedis. Je lisais quand le téléphone a sonné. (3) Je voudrais un croissant, s'il vous plaît.

## Pronouns: le, lui, y, en

Object pronouns replace nouns so you do not repeat yourself, and unlike English they go **before the verb**: "I see him" is **Je le vois**, literally "I him see". This one reordering is the biggest hurdle in French sentence structure, and the payoff is huge: natural-sounding answers.

### Direct objects: le, la, les

A direct object receives the action with no preposition. **me, te, le/la, nous, vous, les** (me, te, le, la become **m', t', l'** before a vowel).

- **Tu vois Paul ? - Oui, je le vois.** - Do you see Paul? - Yes, I see him.
- **Tu aimes la musique ? - Oui, je l'aime.**
- **Tu regardes les infos ? - Non, je ne les regarde pas.** (negation wraps pronoun and verb together)

### Indirect objects: lui, leur

An indirect object replaces **à + a person**. **me, te, lui, nous, vous, leur**. **Lui** is both "to him" and "to her".

- **Je parle à Marie. → Je lui parle.** - I'm talking to her.
- **Il téléphone à ses parents. → Il leur téléphone.**

Watch the verbs where English and French disagree. **regarder** (look *at*), **écouter** (listen *to*), **attendre** (wait *for*), **chercher** (look *for*) take a direct object in French: **Je l'attends.** - I'm waiting for him/her. Meanwhile **téléphoner à**, **répondre à**, **demander à**, **dire à**, **donner à** take an indirect one: **Je lui réponds.** - I answer him/her.

### Y and en

- **y** replaces **à + a thing or place** (and usually any place with **à, en, dans, chez**...): **Tu vas à Paris ? - Oui, j'y vais.** - Yes, I'm going (there). **Je pense à l'examen. → J'y pense.** The fixed phrases **Il y a** (there is) and **On y va !** (Let's go!) both use it.
- **en** replaces **de + a thing**, and anything introduced by **du, de la, des** or a number or quantity: **Tu veux du café ? - Oui, j'en veux.** - Yes, I want some. **Tu as des frères ? - Oui, j'en ai deux.** - Yes, I have two (of them). With numbers, keep the number at the end; English drops "of them", French cannot drop **en**.

### Stress pronouns

A separate set, **moi, toi, lui, elle, nous, vous, eux, elles**, is used after prepositions and for emphasis: **avec moi** - with me, **chez eux** - at their place, **Moi, je préfère le thé.** - Me, I prefer tea. **Et toi ?** - And you?

### The order when you stack them

When two object pronouns appear together, they follow a fixed order. This diagram is worth memorising:

![A sentence skeleton showing slots in order: subject, ne, me/te/se/nous/vous, le/la/les, lui/leur, y, en, verb, pas](study/french-zero-to-conversational/pronoun-order.svg "Pronoun order before the verb: me/te/se > le/la/les > lui/leur > y > en")

- **Il me le donne.** - He gives it to me.
- **Je le lui ai dit.** - I told him/her (it).
- **Il y en a trois.** - There are three (of them).
- **Je ne lui en ai pas parlé.** - I didn't talk to him/her about it.

In the passé composé, pronouns go before the **auxiliary**: **Je l'ai vu.** - I saw him. With verb + infinitive, they go before the verb they belong to, usually the infinitive: **Je vais le faire.** - I'm going to do it. **Je peux t'aider ?** - Can I help you?

In **affirmative commands** only, pronouns move after the verb with hyphens, and **me/te** become **moi/toi**: **Donne-le-moi !** - Give it to me! **Vas-y !** - Go on! **Regarde-moi.** - Look at me. In negative commands they return to their normal place: **Ne me le donne pas.**

> **EXERCISE:** Replace the part in brackets with a pronoun. (1) Je donne [le livre] [à Paul]. (2) Tu vas [à Paris] ? - Oui... (3) Elle a trois [chats]. (4) Il m'a donné [les clés]. (5) Nous parlons [à nos voisins].
>
> **Answers:** (1) Je le lui donne. (2) Oui, j'y vais. (3) Elle en a trois. (4) Il me les a données (the participle agrees with **les** = les clés, feminine plural, because it comes before the verb). (5) Nous leur parlons.

Note the **et** in 21, 31, 41, 51, 61 (and 71), but not in 81 or 91. **Vingt** is pronounced "veh(n)" but **vingt-deux** is "veh(n)t-duh". The final consonant of **six**, **dix** and **huit** is silent before a consonant (**six livres**, see leevr), pronounced when the number stands alone (sees), and becomes a liaison before a vowel (**six‿amis**, see-zah-mee).

### 70 to 99: counting in twenties

Here French does arithmetic. There is no word for seventy, eighty or ninety in France; you build them from **soixante** (60) and **quatre-vingts** (four twenties).

![Number line from 60 to 99 split into four bands showing how soixante and quatre-vingt combine with 0 to 19](study/french-zero-to-conversational/numbers-60-99.svg "70 to 99: soixante plus 10 to 19, then quatre-vingt (4 x 20) plus 0 to 19")

|---|---|---|
| 70 | **soixante-dix** | 60 + 10 |
| 71 | **soixante et onze** | 60 + 11 |
| 75 | **soixante-quinze** | 60 + 15 |
| 79 | **soixante-dix-neuf** | 60 + 19 |
| 80 | **quatre-vingts** | 4 × 20 |
| 81 | **quatre-vingt-un** | 4 × 20 + 1 (no et, no s) |
| 88 | **quatre-vingt-huit** | 4 × 20 + 8 |
| 90 | **quatre-vingt-dix** | 4 × 20 + 10 |
| 97 | **quatre-vingt-dix-sept** | 4 × 20 + 17 |
| 99 | **quatre-vingt-dix-neuf** | 4 × 20 + 19 |
| 100 | **cent** | |
| 200 | **deux cents** | (s only when nothing follows) |
| 250 | **deux cent cinquante** | |
| 1 000 | **mille** | (never takes s) |
| 2026 | **deux mille vingt-six** | |

In **Belgium** and **Switzerland**, life is simpler: **septante** (70) and **nonante** (90), and in some Swiss cantons **huitante** (80). You will be understood using either system anywhere. Also note French number formatting: a space groups thousands and a comma marks decimals, so **1 500,50 €** is one thousand five hundred euros and fifty cents. The 1990 spelling reform also allows hyphens throughout (**vingt-et-un**, **deux-cent-cinquante**), and you will see both.

### Dates

Days and months are **lowercase** and masculine. **lundi, mardi, mercredi, jeudi, vendredi, samedi, dimanche**. **janvier, février, mars, avril, mai, juin, juillet, août, septembre, octobre, novembre, décembre**.

- Dates use cardinal numbers with **le**, except the first: **le 14 juillet**, **le premier mai**. **On est le combien aujourd'hui ? - On est le 19 septembre.** - What's the date today?
- **en** + month or year: **en septembre**, **en 2026**. Seasons: **au printemps**, **en été**, **en automne**, **en hiver**.
- **lundi** = on Monday (this one); **le lundi** = on Mondays (every week).
- Dates are written day/month/year: **19/09/2026**.

### Telling the time

**Quelle heure est-il ?** (formal) or **Il est quelle heure ?** (casual) - What time is it? The answer always uses **Il est... heure(s)**.

| Time | Everyday | 24-hour (timetables, formal) |
|---|---|---|
| 1:00 | **Il est une heure.** | une heure |
| 3:15 | **Il est trois heures et quart.** | trois heures quinze |
| 3:30 | **Il est trois heures et demie.** | trois heures trente |
| 3:45 | **Il est quatre heures moins le quart.** | trois heures quarante-cinq |
| 4:50 | **Il est cinq heures moins dix.** | quatre heures cinquante |
| 12:00 | **Il est midi.** / **Il est minuit.** | douze heures / zéro heure |
| 14:30 | **Il est deux heures et demie.** | quatorze heures trente |

The 24-hour clock is standard in France for anything scheduled, and times are written **14 h 30** or **14h30**. "At" is **à**: **Le film commence à 20 h.** - The film starts at 8 pm. After **midi** and **minuit**, the traditional spelling is **et demi** without e: **midi et demi**.

>
> **Answers:** (1) soixante et onze, quatre-vingts, quatre-vingt-quatre, quatre-vingt-seize, mille neuf cent quatre-vingt-dix-neuf. (2) Il est quatorze heures quarante-cinq / Il est trois heures moins le quart. (3) le samedi, en juin.

| Speaker | French | English |

### Weather

French uses **il fait** for most weather: **Il fait beau** - it's nice, **il fait mauvais** - it's bad, **il fait chaud / froid** - it's hot / cold, **il fait 25 degrés**. Some use their own verbs: **il pleut** - it's raining, **il neige** - it's snowing. Others use **il y a**: **il y a du soleil / du vent / des nuages** - it's sunny / windy / cloudy. **Quel temps fait-il ?** - What's the weather like? **la météo** - the weather forecast.

### Dialogue 5: Monday morning, talking about the weekend

| Speaker | French | English |
|---|---|---|
| Hugo | Alors, ton week-end, c'était comment ? | So, how was your weekend? |
| Emma | Super ! Samedi, je suis allée à la plage avec ma sœur. Il faisait très beau. | Great! On Saturday I went to the beach with my sister. The weather was lovely. |
| Hugo | Vous avez nagé ? | Did you swim? |
| Emma | Oui, mais l'eau était froide ! Et toi, qu'est-ce que tu as fait ? | Yes, but the water was cold! And you, what did you do? |
| Hugo | Pas grand-chose. J'ai regardé des séries et j'ai fait mes devoirs. Dimanche, il pleuvait, alors je suis resté chez moi. | Not much. I watched some series and did my homework. On Sunday it was raining, so I stayed home. |
| Emma | Tu as fini le projet d'histoire ? | Have you finished the history project? |
| Hugo | Presque. Je le finirai demain. | Almost. I'll finish it tomorrow. |

## Real spoken French, common mistakes, and culture

### How people actually talk

Textbook French and street French differ, and at A2 you need to *understand* the spoken forms even if you write the standard ones.

| Written / textbook | Spoken | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Nous allons au cinéma.** | **On va au ciné.** | We're going to the movies. (**on** replaces **nous** almost everywhere in speech) |
| **Je ne sais pas.** | **Je sais pas / Chais pas.** | I don't know. (**ne** is dropped constantly) |
| **Il n'y a pas de problème.** | **Y a pas de souci.** | No problem. |
| **Il faut partir.** | **Faut partir.** | We have to go. |
| **Tu es où ?** | **T'es où ?** | Where are you? |
| **Cela** | **Ça** | That |
| **Qu'est-ce que c'est ?** | **C'est quoi ?** | What is it? |
| **Oui** | **Ouais** | Yeah |

Common fillers and small words: **bah** / **ben** (well...), **euh** (um), **du coup** (so, as a result; overused by everyone), **genre** (like), **en fait** (actually), **bref** (anyway), **quoi** at the end of a sentence for emphasis (**C'était bizarre, quoi.**). Intensifiers: **trop** (really: **c'est trop bien** - it's really good), **grave** (totally, slang). Young people use **verlan**, syllable-reversed slang: **meuf** (from **femme**) - woman/girlfriend, **relou** (from **lourd**) - annoying, **ouf** (from **fou**) - crazy. Recognise them; use them sparingly until you are sure of the register.

> **TIP:** Use **on** in your own speech from day one. It takes the easy third-person verb form (**on va**, **on fait**, **on a**) and it sounds natural. Keep **nous** for writing.

### The twelve most common beginner mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Je suis 16 ans. | **J'ai 16 ans.** | Age uses avoir. |
| Je suis chaud. | **J'ai chaud.** | "Je suis chaud" means "I'm up for it" or worse. |
| Je suis fini. | **J'ai fini.** | "Je suis fini" means "I'm done for". |
| Je suis plein. | **J'ai bien mangé / Je n'ai plus faim.** | "Plein" for a person sounds like drunk or (for an animal) pregnant. |
| J'attends pour le bus. | **J'attends le bus.** | attendre = to wait for; no preposition. |
| J'ai visité ma grand-mère. | **J'ai rendu visite à ma grand-mère.** | visiter is for places; people take **rendre visite à**. |
| Je suis excité pour le concert. | **J'ai hâte d'aller au concert.** | "excité" leans towards overexcited or aroused. |
| Il est un médecin. | **Il est médecin. / C'est un médecin.** | No article after il/elle est + profession. |
| J'ai étudié français pour deux ans. (still studying) | **J'étudie le français depuis deux ans.** | Ongoing action: present + depuis. |
| Je veux un café. (to a waiter) | **Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît.** | Conditional is the polite register. |
| Le personne / la problème | **la personne / le problème** | Gender is per word; words in -ème from Greek are often masculine. |
| C'est très bon film. | **C'est un très bon film.** | Nouns almost always need an article. |

### Culture notes

- **Francophonie.** French is spoken on five continents by more than 320 million people, according to the Organisation internationale de la Francophonie. Most French speakers now live in Africa. Accents and vocabulary vary: Quebec, Belgium, Switzerland, Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire all have their own flavour. Standard textbook French is understood everywhere.
- **Meals are sacred.** Lunch in France typically runs from about noon to 2 pm and dinner often starts around 8 pm. People rarely eat while walking, and meals can last a long time. The **goûter** (after-school snack around 4 pm) is a children's institution.
- **Bread and the baguette.** Say **Une baguette, s'il vous plaît** at the **boulangerie**. The **baguette tradition** (**une tradi**) is the premium version. In 2022, UNESCO added the craft and culture of the baguette to its list of intangible cultural heritage.
- **Politeness formulas.** Beyond **bonjour**, emails and letters use formal openings and closings (**Madame, Monsieur**, and **Cordialement** as a neutral sign-off). Using **vous** with a teacher or a boss is a sign of respect, not coldness.
- **School in France.** **l'école primaire** (6 to 11), **le collège** (11 to 15, ending with the **brevet** exam), **le lycée** (15 to 18, ending with the **baccalauréat**, usually just **le bac**). Grades are out of 20; 10 is a pass, 14 is very good, and 18 or more is exceptional.
- **Strikes and debate.** Arguing is a national sport, and disagreement in conversation is often a sign of interest, not hostility. A lively **débat** at dinner is normal.

### Spaced repetition that works

- **Sentences over single words.** A card like **Il y a du vent aujourd'hui.** - It's windy today teaches vocabulary, the partitive and a weather idiom at once.
- **Gender is part of the word.** Front: "the cheese". Back: **le fromage**. Colour-code masculine and feminine if it helps you.
- **Add audio** from a native recording (Forvo, or a text-to-speech voice) so you never learn a wrong pronunciation.
- **Production cards for key verbs.** Front: "nous (prendre), present". Back: **prenons**. Do this for the irregular nine in every tense you learn.
- **Never skip a day of reviews.** Skip new cards if you must, but reviews pile up fast and the backlog is the number one reason people quit Anki.

> **TIP:** At the end of week 12 and week 16, test yourself against the official CEFR descriptors or take a free practice test (TV5MONDE has TCF-style practice, and France Éducation international publishes sample DELF papers). If you want a certificate, the **DELF A2** and **DELF B1** exams are recognised worldwide and never expire.

## Cheat sheet

**Sounds.** u (tü) vs ou (voo). Nasals: an/en (ah(n)), on (oh(n)), in/ain (eh(n)); n/m + vowel or doubled = not nasal (bon/bonne). Final consonants silent except often c, r, f, l. -ent verb ending silent. H always silent; h aspiré blocks linking.

**Linking.** Elision: le/la/je/me/te/se/ne/de/que → l', j', m'... Liaison required after determiners, subject pronouns, BAGS adjectives, short prepositions; forbidden after **et**, before h aspiré, after singular nouns. s/x → z, d → t.

**Articles.** le/la/l'/les (specific, general, after aimer/préférer). un/une/des (countable). du/de la/de l'/des (some, uncountable). After negation or quantities → **de**. à + le = au, à + les = aux, de + le = du, de + les = des.

**Adjectives.** Agree: +e feminine, +s plural. After the noun, except BAGS (beauty, age, goodness, size). beau/bel/belle, nouveau/nouvel/nouvelle, vieux/vieil/vieille.

**Present.** -er: e, es, e, ons, ez, ent. -ir: is, is, it, issons, issez, issent. -re: s, s, -, ons, ez, ent. être: suis, es, est, sommes, êtes, sont. avoir: ai, as, a, avons, avez, ont. aller: vais, vas, va, allons, allez, vont. faire: fais, fais, fait, faisons, faites, font.

**Negation and questions.** ne + verb + pas/jamais/plus/rien/personne/que. Questions: intonation < est-ce que < inversion (Parle-t-il ?). Answer yes to a negative question with **si**.

**Tenses.**

| Tense | Formation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Near future | aller + infinitive | Je vais partir. |
| Passé composé | avoir/être + participle | J'ai mangé. Elle est partie. |
| Imparfait | nous-stem + ais, ais, ait, ions, iez, aient | Je mangeais. |
| Futur simple | infinitive + ai, as, a, ons, ez, ont | Je mangerai. |
| Conditionnel | future stem + imparfait endings | Je voudrais. |

**Être verbs.** aller/venir, arriver/partir, entrer/sortir, monter/descendre, naître/mourir, rester, tomber, retourner, rentrer, devenir, revenir, passer, plus all reflexive verbs. Participle agrees with the subject.

**PC vs imparfait.** Event, sequence, one-off = PC. Background, description, in progress, habit = imparfait.

**Pronoun order.** me/te/se/nous/vous > le/la/les > lui/leur > y > en > verb. Before the auxiliary in PC. After the verb only in affirmative commands (Donne-le-moi).

**Numbers.** 70 = soixante-dix, 71 = soixante et onze, 80 = quatre-vingts, 81 = quatre-vingt-un, 90 = quatre-vingt-dix, 99 = quatre-vingt-dix-neuf. Belgium/Switzerland: septante, nonante.

**Survival.** Bonjour first, always. Vous by default. Je voudrais... s'il vous plaît. Vous pouvez répéter ? Plus lentement. Comment dit-on... ?

## Where to go next

Once you can hold a slow ten-minute conversation about yourself, your week and your plans, you are at A2. From here to B1 and beyond, the recipe shifts: much more listening and reading for pleasure, regular conversation, and grammar only as needed (the subjunctive, relative pronouns **qui/que/dont/où**, the plus-que-parfait, and more idiomatic pronoun use are the next big topics).

Free resources that exist and are worth your time at the time of writing (2026); check each site for current terms:

- **TV5MONDE EDU** (apprendre.tv5monde.com, plus an app formerly called "Apprendre"): thousands of free video-based exercises sorted by CEFR level, with a beginner course translated into English and TCF practice tests.
- **RFI Français facile** (francaisfacile.rfi.fr): the daily **Journal en français facile**, a ten-minute news bulletin in simple French with full transcripts and weekly exercises. Ideal from late A2.
- **Language Transfer: Complete French** (languagetransfer.org): a free audio course that builds sentences by thinking, leaning heavily on cognates. Excellent for weeks 1 to 8.
- **Coffee Break French** (coffeebreaklanguages.com): free podcast lessons; Season 1 is aimed at absolute beginners. Paid extras exist but the audio is free.
- **Easy French** (YouTube): unscripted street interviews with French and English subtitles, plus slower "Super Easy French" episodes for beginners.
- **Lawless French** (lawlessfrench.com): a free reference site with thousands of grammar, vocabulary and pronunciation lessons sorted by CEFR level.
- **innerFrench** (podcast): intermediate podcast in clear, natural French. It ended in 2026 after nine years, but the archive remains available and is perfect for the jump from A2 to B1.
- **Anki** (apps.ankiweb.net): free spaced-repetition flashcards on Windows, macOS, Linux, Android (AnkiDroid) and the web; the iOS app is a one-time purchase.
- **Dictionaries and tools:** WordReference (dictionary with excellent forums), Reverso Context (words in real sentences), Forvo (native pronunciations), Wiktionary (IPA and conjugation tables), Tatoeba (free example sentences with translations), and Le Conjugueur (conjugation tables).
- **Mauril** (mauril.ca): a free, ad-free app from CBC/Radio-Canada built on real Canadian TV and radio content. Only available in Canada.
- **Duolingo**: fine as a daily habit and for vocabulary exposure, but pair it with a real grammar resource and real listening; on its own it will not get you to conversation.

Paid options worth considering once the free ones run dry: an online tutor for 30 minutes twice a week (italki, Preply and similar marketplaces have French tutors at a wide range of prices), and a graded reader series matched to your level. Your concrete next steps: finish the 16-week plan, sit a free A2 practice test, book the **DELF A2** if you want a certificate, and start watching one French series with French subtitles. **Bon courage !** - Good luck, and keep going!
