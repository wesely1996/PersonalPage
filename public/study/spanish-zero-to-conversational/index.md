Spanish is the second most widely spoken native language on Earth, with roughly 500 million native speakers, and it is one of the friendliest languages an English speaker can pick up. Its spelling is almost perfectly phonetic, thousands of words look like English words, and its grammar, while richer than English, is regular enough that a few dozen patterns carry you a long way. This guide takes you from absolute zero to the level of a good high-school student after a few years of classes: roughly CEFR A2, touching B1. At that level you can order food, ask for directions, talk about your family, your week and your plans, tell a story about last summer, and hold a slow but real 20-minute conversation with a patient native speaker.

The guide follows the order most learners actually need things in. Each section explains *why* the grammar works the way it does, gives you tables to look back at, and ends with an exercise. Answers are included so you can check yourself. Pronunciation hints use capital letters for the stressed syllable, for example `hola (OH-lah) - hello`. Because Spanish spelling is phonetic, hints are given for every word in the early sections and afterwards only where a word is tricky; by then you will be able to read the spelling itself.

## Before you start: time, method and free words

### How long does it take?

The US Foreign Service Institute (FSI), which has trained American diplomats for decades, puts Spanish in **Category I**, the easiest group for English speakers, alongside French, Italian, Portuguese and Dutch. Its estimate for reaching "professional working proficiency" (roughly B2/C1) is **24-30 weeks of full-time study, about 600-750 class hours**, plus a lot of homework. Those are intensive-classroom hours with small classes and daily tutoring, so treat them as a floor, not a promise.

For the goal of this guide, a realistic picture looks like this:

| Target | Rough total hours | At 1 hour/day | At 1.5 hours/day |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 (survival) | 80-100 | ~3 months | ~2 months |
| A2 (everyday topics) | 180-200 | ~6 months | ~4 months |
| B1 (independent, conversational) | 350-400 | ~12 months | ~8 months |

These are guided-learning estimates commonly quoted for European languages; your mileage depends on how much of the time is active (speaking, writing, retrieving from memory) versus passive (watching a video with English subtitles). The 16-week plan at the end of this guide asks for about 1.5 hours a day and gets you to a solid A2 with B1 moments on familiar topics. Reaching a full B1 takes a few more months of the same habits.

### What CEFR levels mean in practice

The Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) describes six levels, A1 to C2. Spanish exams such as the Instituto Cervantes **DELE** and the online **SIELE** are aligned to it.

| Level | You can... |
|---|---|
| A1 | introduce yourself, ask and answer simple questions about where you live and what you have, if people speak slowly |
| A2 | handle routine exchanges: shopping, directions, family, work or school, simple past events |
| B1 | deal with most travel situations, describe experiences, hopes and plans, give short reasons for opinions |
| B2 | talk fluently and spontaneously with natives without strain on either side |

### How to study (what actually works)

1. **Input first, lots of it.** You learn a language mostly by understanding messages in it. Aim for listening and reading you understand at about 80-90 percent. Graded podcasts and "comprehensible input" videos are ideal for this.
2. **Retrieve, do not just re-read.** Flashcards with spaced repetition (Anki) beat rereading notes because they force you to pull the word out of memory right before you would forget it. Make cards Spanish-to-English *and* English-to-Spanish, and put words inside short sentences.
3. **Speak early, badly and often.** From week 2, talk to yourself out loud: describe what you are doing, narrate your day. From week 4, get a conversation partner (a tutor, an exchange partner, a friend).
4. **Learn chunks, not just words.** `¿Me pone un café?` (meh POH-neh oon kah-FEH) - "Could I get a coffee?" (Spain) is more useful as one unit than as four words to assemble.
5. **Shadow.** Play a short native clip, pause, and repeat it with exactly the same rhythm and melody. This is the fastest way to fix your accent.
6. **Grammar as a map, not a destination.** Read a grammar point, do a few exercises, then go find it in real input. You understand `me gusta` long before you can explain it.

> **TIP:** Keep the daily routine boring and fixed: 15 minutes of Anki, 30-45 minutes of input, 15-20 minutes of grammar or writing, 10+ minutes of speaking. Consistency beats weekend marathons, because memory consolidation happens during sleep.

### Cognates: thousands of words for free

Spanish and English share a huge Latin-derived vocabulary, much of it through French and Latin borrowings into English. Learn the conversion patterns and you instantly own thousands of words:

| English ending | Spanish ending | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -tion | -ción | nación, información, educación |
| -ty | -dad / -tad | universidad, ciudad, libertad |
| -ly | -mente | rápidamente, finalmente, normalmente |
| -ous | -oso | famoso, delicioso, nervioso |
| -ic / -ical | -ico | público, histórico, música |
| -ist | -ista | artista, turista, dentista |
| -ent / -ant | -ente / -ante | presidente, importante, elegante |
| -ize | -izar | organizar, realizar, visualizar |

Nouns ending in `-ción`, `-dad` and `-tad` are feminine: `la nación`, `la ciudad`. Keep that in mind for later.

> **WARNING:** Not every look-alike means the same thing. `Actual` means "current", `realizar` means "to carry out" (not "to realize"), and `embarazada` means "pregnant", not "embarrassed". The full list of traps is in the mistakes section.

> **EXERCISE:** Convert these English words into Spanish using the patterns above: *nation, curiosity, generous, probably, fantastic, dentist, different, to organize.*
>
> *Answers:* nación, curiosidad, generoso, probablemente, fantástico, dentista, diferente, organizar.

## Sounds, spelling and accents

Spanish spelling is close to one letter, one sound. Once you know the rules you can pronounce any word you see and spell almost any word you hear. That is a huge advantage over English, so invest the first week in getting the sounds right; bad habits formed now take months to undo.

![The five pure vowels and a table of the consonants that trip up English speakers](study/spanish-zero-to-conversational/pronunciation-map.svg "Five pure vowels and ten consonant traps: learn this page and you can read Spanish aloud")

### The five vowels

English has around a dozen vowel sounds and loves to "glide" them: English "no" is really "noh-oo". Spanish has exactly five vowels, and they are short, tense and pure. They also keep their quality when unstressed: the English "uh" sound (schwa) does not exist.

| Vowel | Sound | Example | Hint | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a | "ah" in father | casa | KAH-sah | house |
| e | "eh" in bet | mesa | MEH-sah | table |
| i | "ee" in see | sí | see | yes |
| o | "oh", clipped | poco | POH-koh | little, not much |
| u | "oo" in moon | luna | LOO-nah | moon |

When two vowels meet, weak vowels (`i`, `u`) glide into strong ones (`a`, `e`, `o`): `bueno` (BWEH-noh) - good, `siete` (SYEH-teh) - seven, `aire` (EYE-reh) - air.

### Consonants that differ from English

- **r vs rr.** A single `r` between vowels or at the end of a syllable is one quick tap of the tongue, the same sound as the "tt" in American "butter": `pero` (PEH-roh) - but. Double `rr`, and `r` at the start of a word or after `n`, `l` or `s`, is a trill: several fast taps. `perro` (PEH-rroh) - dog. `pero` and `perro` are different words, so the distinction matters.
- **j, and g before e or i.** A rough breathy "h" from the back of the throat, like Scottish "loch". Softer in the Caribbean and much of Latin America. `jamón` (hah-MOHN) - ham, `gente` (HEN-teh) - people.
- **g before a, o, u** is the hard "g" of "go": `gato` (GAH-toh) - cat. To get a hard g before e or i, Spanish inserts a silent u: `guerra` (GEH-rrah) - war, `guitarra` (gee-TAH-rrah) - guitar. When you need to hear that u, it gets two dots: `pingüino` (peen-GWEE-noh) - penguin.
- **c, z, s.** `c` before a, o, u is "k": `casa`. `c` before e, i and every `z` is where regions differ: most of Spain pronounces them like English "th" in "thin" (called **distinción**), while Latin America, the Canary Islands and parts of southern Spain pronounce them as "s" (**seseo**). `cena` - dinner is THEH-nah in Madrid and SEH-nah in Mexico City. Both are correct.
- **ll and y.** In most places both sound like the "y" in "yes" (sometimes closer to the "j" in "jam"): `llamo` (YAH-moh) - I call. In Argentina and Uruguay they sound like "sh" or "zh": `calle` (KAH-sheh) - street.
- **ñ** is its own letter, "ny" as in "canyon": `año` (AH-nyoh) - year. Do not drop the tilde: `ano` means "anus".
- **b and v** are the same sound. At the start of a phrase or after m/n it is a normal "b"; between vowels the lips barely touch: `vivir` (bee-BEER) - to live. Natives spell-check each other with "b de burro" and "v de vaca".
- **h** is always silent: `hola` (OH-lah) - hello, `hay` (eye) - there is. The combination `ch` is the "ch" of "church".
- **qu** before e or i is "k" with a silent u: `queso` (KEH-soh) - cheese, `quiero` (KYEH-roh) - I want.
- **d** between vowels softens toward the "th" in "this": `nada` (NAH-thah, very soft) - nothing. **t** and **p** have no puff of air, unlike English.

### Stress and the written accent

Every Spanish word has one stressed syllable, and the spelling tells you which one. Three rules:

1. Words ending in a **vowel, n or s** are stressed on the **next-to-last** syllable: `CA-sa`, `HA-blan`, `ZA-pa-tos`.
2. Words ending in **any other consonant** are stressed on the **last** syllable: `ha-BLAR`, `ciu-DAD`, `es-pa-ÑOL`.
3. Anything that breaks rules 1 or 2 carries a **written accent (tilde)** on the stressed vowel: `can-CIÓN`, `LÁ-piz`, `te-LÉ-fo-no`, `ÁR-bol`.

So `canción` needs an accent (ends in n, but stress is last), while its plural `canciones` does not (ends in s, stress is next-to-last). Accents also distinguish pairs of short words that would otherwise look identical:

| Without accent | With accent |
|---|---|
| tu - your | tú - you |
| el - the | él - he |
| mi - my | mí - me (after prepositions) |
| si - if | sí - yes |
| te - you (object) | té - tea |
| se - (reflexive pronoun) | sé - I know |
| de - of, from | dé - give (subjunctive) |
| mas - but (literary) | más - more |

Question and exclamation words always carry an accent: `qué, quién, dónde, cuándo, cómo, cuánto, cuál`. And Spanish opens questions and exclamations with an upside-down mark: `¿Cómo estás?` - How are you? `¡Qué bien!` - How great! On a phone keyboard, long-press `?` and `!`; on Windows, the US-International layout gives you `'` + vowel for accents, `~` + n for ñ, and AltGr for ¿ and ¡.

> **EXERCISE:** Mark the stressed syllable and decide whether each word needs a written accent: *lapiz, reloj, examen, examenes, arbol, cafe, joven, jovenes, facil, ciudad.*
>
> *Answers:* LÁ-piz (lápiz, accent), re-LOJ (no accent), e-XA-men (no accent), e-XÁ-me-nes (exámenes, accent), ÁR-bol (árbol, accent), ca-FÉ (café, accent), JO-ven (no accent), JÓ-ve-nes (jóvenes, accent), FÁ-cil (fácil, accent), ciu-DAD (no accent).

### One language, many accents

About nine in ten native speakers live in the Americas. Spanish is official in 20 countries plus Puerto Rico, and the United States has well over 40 million people who speak it at home. Educated speakers from Madrid, Mexico City, Bogotá and Buenos Aires understand each other without difficulty; the differences are about as large as those between British, American and Australian English.

![Schematic layout of Spanish dialect zones with signature features for each](study/spanish-zero-to-conversational/dialect-map.svg "Dialect zones (schematic, not to scale): copy one accent, learn to recognize the rest")

The differences you will actually notice as a learner:

- **vosotros vs ustedes.** Spain uses `vosotros` (boh-SOH-trohs) for informal "you all" and `ustedes` for formal "you all". Latin America uses `ustedes` for both. In this guide, vosotros forms appear in tables so you can recognize them; if you are learning Latin American Spanish you can skip producing them.
- **Voseo.** In Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay and much of Central America, `vos` replaces `tú` with its own verb forms: `vos sos` - you are (instead of `tú eres`), `vos tenés` - you have (instead of `tú tienes`), `vos hablás` - you speak. You will understand it instantly once you have heard it a few times. Learn `tú` first; it is understood everywhere.
- **Pronunciation:** distinción (Spain) vs seseo (everywhere else), `ll/y` as "sh" in the Río de la Plata, and weakened or dropped final `s` in the Caribbean, Andalusia and Chile (`¿cómo estás?` sounds like "¿cómo estáh?").
- **Past tense habits:** in most of Spain, events of "today" use the present perfect: `Hoy he comido paella` - Today I have eaten / I ate paella. Most of Latin America prefers the simple preterite: `Hoy comí paella`.
- **Vocabulary.** Everyday objects vary the way "elevator/lift" or "truck/lorry" do in English:

| English | Spain | Mexico | Argentina / others |
|---|---|---|---|
| car | coche | carro, coche | auto (AR), carro (CO, VE) |
| computer | ordenador | computadora | computadora |
| cell phone | móvil | celular | celular |
| bus | autobús | camión (city bus) | colectivo (AR), guagua (Caribbean) |
| juice | zumo | jugo | jugo |
| potato | patata | papa | papa |
| apartment | piso | departamento | departamento (AR), apartamento |
| to drive | conducir | manejar | manejar |
| cool! | ¡qué guay! | ¡qué padre! / ¡qué chido! | ¡qué bueno! / ¡qué chévere! (CO, VE) |
| OK | vale | sale / órale | dale (AR, Caribbean) |

> **WARNING:** `coger` means "to take/catch" in Spain (`coger el autobús`) but is a vulgar sexual word in Mexico, Argentina and much of Latin America. Use `tomar el autobús` and you are safe everywhere.

> **TIP:** Pick one variety to imitate (the one you are most likely to use, or the one whose media you enjoy) and be consistent with pronunciation. Then deliberately listen to other accents so they never surprise you.

`Encantado` is said by a man, `encantada` by a woman: the adjective describes the speaker. Your first grammar lesson hides inside a greeting.

### Tú or usted?

Spanish has two ways of saying "you" to one person:

- **tú** (too) - informal: friends, family, children, classmates, people your age in casual settings.
- **usted** (oos-TEHD, written Ud.) - formal: older strangers, officials, a doctor, a customer in many shops, your friend's grandmother.

`Usted` historically comes from *vuestra merced*, "your grace", which is why it takes **third-person** verb forms, the same as "he/she": `¿Cómo está usted?` - How are you (formal)? versus `¿Cómo estás?` (tú).

Usage varies. Spain is quick to use `tú`, even with strangers of similar age. Colombia and parts of Central America use `usted` even between close friends and family. When in doubt, start with `usted` and let the other person invite you to switch: if someone says `puedes tutearme` - you can use tú with me, do it.

> **NOTE:** You do not need the subject pronoun most of the time. The verb ending already says who is acting: `hablo` can only mean "I speak". Spanish speakers add `yo`, `tú`, `él` for emphasis or contrast: `Yo hablo inglés, y tú hablas español`. Beginners who say `yo` in every sentence sound robotic.

### Subject pronouns

| Person | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | yo - I | nosotros / nosotras - we |
| 2nd informal | tú - you (vos in some regions) | vosotros / vosotras - you all (Spain only) |
| 2nd formal | usted (Ud.) - you | ustedes (Uds.) - you all |
| 3rd | él - he, ella - she | ellos - they (masc. or mixed), ellas - they (fem.) |

A group of ten women and one man is `ellos`: the masculine plural is the default for mixed groups.

> **EXERCISE:** Would you use tú or usted? (1) Your host mother, who is 60, on the first day. (2) A 20-year-old in a hostel in Barcelona. (3) A police officer. (4) Your new classmate. (5) A shop assistant in Bogotá.
>
> *Answers:* (1) usted, until invited to switch. (2) tú. (3) usted. (4) tú. (5) usted is the safe default; Colombians use it widely.

## Nouns, articles and adjectives

### Every noun has a gender

Every Spanish noun is either masculine or feminine, even objects and ideas. The gender is grammatical, not about meaning: `la mesa` - the table is feminine, `el libro` - the book is masculine. You learn it with the article, as one unit.

Patterns that cover most nouns:

| Usually masculine | Usually feminine |
|---|---|
| ends in -o: el libro, el vaso | ends in -a: la casa, la mesa |
| ends in -or: el color, el amor | ends in -ción, -sión: la canción, la televisión |
| ends in -aje: el viaje, el garaje | ends in -dad, -tad, -tud: la ciudad, la libertad, la actitud |
| Greek -ma words: el problema, el sistema, el idioma, el tema | ends in -ez, -umbre: la vez, la costumbre |

Famous exceptions: `el día` - the day, `el mapa` - the map, `el sofá`, `la mano` - the hand, `la foto` (short for fotografía), `la moto`, `la radio`. Nouns ending in -e or other consonants must simply be learned: `el coche`, `la noche`, `el lápiz`, `la luz`.

Feminine nouns starting with a stressed `a-` or `ha-` take `el` in the singular just for pronunciation, but stay feminine: `el agua fría` - the cold water, `las aguas`. `El hambre` - hunger works the same way.

### Articles

| | Masc. sing. | Fem. sing. | Masc. pl. | Fem. pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| the | el | la | los | las |
| a, some | un | una | unos | unas |

Two contractions are mandatory: `a + el = al` (`Voy al cine` - I'm going to the cinema) and `de + el = del` (`la casa del profesor` - the teacher's house). They do not happen with `él` - he: `de él`.

Spanish uses the definite article more than English, for things in general and for abstract nouns: `Me gusta el café` - I like coffee. `La vida es bella` - Life is beautiful. It is also used with days (`el lunes` - on Monday) and with titles when talking about someone (`el señor García`).

### Plurals

- Ends in a vowel: add **-s**: `libro -> libros`, `café -> cafés`.
- Ends in a consonant: add **-es**: `ciudad -> ciudades`, `profesor -> profesores`.
- Final **-z** becomes **-ces**: `lápiz -> lápices`, `vez -> veces`.
- Watch accents shift because of the stress rules: `canción -> canciones`, `joven -> jóvenes`.

### Adjective agreement and position

Adjectives match the noun in gender and number, and normally come **after** it:

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| -o/-a adjectives | el chico alto, la chica alta | los chicos altos, las chicas altas |
| -e adjectives | el libro grande, la casa grande | los libros grandes, las casas grandes |
| consonant adjectives | el coche azul, la mesa azul | los coches azules, las mesas azules |
| nationality | el chico español, la chica española | los chicos españoles, las chicas españolas |

Some common adjectives go before the noun, and a few shorten there: `bueno -> buen` (`un buen amigo` - a good friend), `malo -> mal`, and `grande -> gran`, which also changes meaning: `un gran hombre` - a great man, `un hombre grande` - a big man. Numbers and quantity words (`mucho`, `poco`, `otro`) also go before: `muchos amigos` - many friends, `otra vez` - again (never `una otra`).

Possessives: `mi(s)` - my, `tu(s)` - your, `su(s)` - his/her/their/your (formal), `nuestro/a(s)` - our, `vuestro/a(s)` - your (vosotros). They agree with the thing owned, not the owner: `mis hermanas` - my sisters, `nuestra casa` - our house.

## The present tense

The present tense in Spanish covers "I speak", "I am speaking" and often "I will speak" (`Mañana voy a Madrid` - I'm going to Madrid tomorrow). It is where most of your first months of speaking happen.

### Regular verbs: -ar, -er, -ir

Every infinitive ends in `-ar`, `-er` or `-ir`. Chop off those two letters to get the stem, then add endings:

| | hablar - to speak | comer - to eat | vivir - to live |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | hablo | como | vivo |
| tú | hablas | comes | vives |
| él / ella / usted | habla | come | vive |
| nosotros | hablamos | comemos | vivimos |
| vosotros | habláis | coméis | vivís |
| ellos / ellas / ustedes | hablan | comen | viven |

Notice the logic: `-ar` verbs use the vowel **a**, `-er` and `-ir` verbs use **e**, and `-ir` differs from `-er` only in the nosotros and vosotros forms. The `-s` for tú, the `-mos` for nosotros and the `-n` for ellos run through almost every tense you will learn, so learning them once pays off repeatedly.

High-frequency regular verbs: `trabajar` - to work, `estudiar` - to study, `escuchar` - to listen, `mirar` - to look at, `llegar` - to arrive, `necesitar` - to need, `tomar` - to take/drink, `comprar` - to buy, `beber` - to drink, `leer` - to read, `aprender` - to learn, `correr` - to run, `escribir` - to write, `abrir` - to open, `vivir` - to live, `compartir` - to share.

### Stem-changing "boot" verbs

Many common verbs change the vowel in their stem whenever that vowel is stressed. The stress falls on the stem in yo, tú, él and ellos forms, and on the ending in nosotros and vosotros. Draw a line around the changing forms in a conjugation table and you get the shape of a boot, which is why teachers call them **boot verbs**.

![Three conjugation grids with a boot outline around the forms whose stem changes](study/spanish-zero-to-conversational/boot-verbs.svg "Boot verbs: e -> ie, o -> ue and e -> i, but never in nosotros or vosotros")

| e -> ie | o -> ue | e -> i (-ir only) |
|---|---|---|
| querer - to want, love: quiero | poder - can: puedo | pedir - to ask for: pido |
| pensar - to think: pienso | dormir - to sleep: duermo | servir - to serve: sirvo |
| empezar - to begin: empiezo | volver - to return: vuelvo | repetir - to repeat: repito |
| entender - to understand: entiendo | encontrar - to find: encuentro | seguir - to follow: sigo |
| preferir - to prefer: prefiero | costar - to cost: cuesta | vestirse - to get dressed: me visto |
| cerrar - to close: cierro | recordar - to remember: recuerdo | |

`Jugar` - to play (a sport or game) is the lone u -> ue verb: `juego, juegas, juega, jugamos, jugáis, juegan`.

There is no reliable way to know from the infinitive whether a verb stem-changes. Dictionaries mark it as `querer (ie)`, `dormir (ue)`; put that marker on your flashcards.

### The irregular verbs you cannot avoid

The most-used verbs are the most irregular, because frequent use protects old forms from being regularized. Learn these ten as pure memorization. Several have an irregular **yo** form (the "yo-go" verbs: tengo, vengo, hago, digo, pongo, salgo) and are otherwise regular or boot verbs.

| | ser - to be | estar - to be | ir - to go | tener - to have | hacer - to do/make |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| yo | soy | estoy | voy | tengo | hago |
| tú | eres | estás | vas | tienes | haces |
| él/ella/Ud. | es | está | va | tiene | hace |
| nosotros | somos | estamos | vamos | tenemos | hacemos |
| vosotros | sois | estáis | vais | tenéis | hacéis |
| ellos/Uds. | son | están | van | tienen | hacen |

| | poder - can | querer - to want | decir - to say | venir - to come | saber - to know |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| yo | puedo | quiero | digo | vengo | sé |
| tú | puedes | quieres | dices | vienes | sabes |
| él/ella/Ud. | puede | quiere | dice | viene | sabe |
| nosotros | podemos | queremos | decimos | venimos | sabemos |
| vosotros | podéis | queréis | decís | venís | sabéis |
| ellos/Uds. | pueden | quieren | dicen | vienen | saben |

Other useful irregular yo forms: `salir -> salgo` (I go out), `poner -> pongo` (I put), `traer -> traigo` (I bring), `ver -> veo` (I see), `dar -> doy` (I give), `conocer -> conozco` (I know, am familiar with), `oír -> oigo` (I hear).

`Tener` does a lot of work that English gives to "to be":

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| tengo 16 años | I am 16 (years old) |
| tengo hambre / sed | I am hungry / thirsty |
| tengo frío / calor | I am cold / hot |
| tengo sueño | I am sleepy |
| tengo miedo | I am afraid |
| tengo prisa | I am in a hurry |
| tengo razón | I am right |
| tengo que estudiar | I have to study |

> **WARNING:** Never say `estoy caliente` to mean "I'm hot (temperature)". With a person it suggests sexual arousal. Use `tengo calor`. Likewise, `soy 16` means nothing; age always uses tener.

### Saber vs conocer

Both mean "to know", but they split the territory:

- **saber** - facts, information, and *how to do something* (+ infinitive): `Sé la respuesta` - I know the answer. `¿Sabes nadar?` - Can you (do you know how to) swim? `No sé dónde está` - I don't know where it is.
- **conocer** - being acquainted with people, places, works: `Conozco a tu hermano` - I know your brother. `¿Conoces Madrid?` - Have you been to / Do you know Madrid?

Notice `a` in `conozco a tu hermano`. Spanish puts this **personal a** before a direct object that is a specific person (or a beloved pet): `Veo a María` - I see María, but `Veo la casa` - I see the house.

> **EXERCISE:** Conjugate: (1) Nosotros (vivir) en Texas. (2) ¿Tú (querer) un café? (3) Ellos no (poder) venir. (4) Yo (hacer) la tarea. (5) Mi hermano (dormir) mucho. (6) Yo (conocer / saber) a tu profesora. (7) ¿(Tú, saber / conocer) cocinar? (8) Yo (tener) 17 años. (9) Vosotros (pedir) pizza. (10) ¿Qué (decir) tú?
>
> *Answers:* (1) vivimos. (2) quieres. (3) pueden. (4) hago. (5) duerme. (6) conozco. (7) Sabes. (8) tengo. (9) pedís (no stem change in vosotros). (10) dices.

## Ser, estar and hay

Spanish has two verbs for "to be", and choosing between them is the most famous beginner problem. The underlying idea: **ser** tells you *what* something is (its identity, category, inherent traits), and **estar** tells you *how* or *where* it is (a state, a condition, a location). Two memory hooks, DOCTOR and PLACE, cover nearly every case.

![Two columns: SER as DOCTOR and ESTAR as PLACE with examples, and adjectives that change meaning](study/spanish-zero-to-conversational/ser-vs-estar.svg "Ser = DOCTOR, estar = PLACE; some adjectives change meaning depending on which you choose")

### Ser = DOCTOR

| Use | Example | English |
|---|---|---|
| **D**escription (inherent) | Es alta y simpática. | She's tall and nice. |
| **O**ccupation | Soy estudiante. / Es médico. | I'm a student. / He's a doctor. |
| **C**haracteristic | Eres muy paciente. | You're very patient. |
| **T**ime and date | Son las tres. Hoy es lunes. | It's three o'clock. Today is Monday. |
| **O**rigin (and material) | Somos de México. La mesa es de madera. | We're from Mexico. The table is (made of) wood. |
| **R**elationship | Ana es mi hermana. | Ana is my sister. |

Notice that occupations drop the article: `Soy estudiante`, not `soy un estudiante` (unless you add an adjective: `Es un buen médico`).

### Estar = PLACE

| Use | Example | English |
|---|---|---|
| **P**osition | Está sentado. | He's sitting (seated). |
| **L**ocation | Lima está en Perú. ¿Dónde estás? | Lima is in Peru. Where are you? |
| **A**ction in progress (+ -ndo) | Estoy comiendo. | I'm eating. |
| **C**ondition | El café está frío. La puerta está abierta. | The coffee is cold. The door is open. |
| **E**motion | Estamos contentos. Está nerviosa. | We're happy. She's nervous. |

The deciding question is often "is this a state the thing is in, or what the thing is?" `La sopa es fría` would mean the soup is a cold soup by nature (like gazpacho); `La sopa está fría` means it has gone cold.

### Same adjective, different meaning

| With ser | With estar |
|---|---|
| es aburrido - he's boring | está aburrido - he's bored |
| es listo - he's clever | está listo - he's ready |
| es malo - he's bad (a bad person/thing) | está malo - he's ill / it tastes bad |
| es rico - he's rich | está rico - it's delicious |
| es verde - it's green (colour) | está verde - it's unripe |
| es orgulloso - he's arrogant | está orgulloso - he's proud (of something) |

Two exceptions to memorize: the **location of events** uses ser (`La fiesta es en mi casa` - The party is at my house; think "takes place"), and **death** uses estar, logic notwithstanding (`Está muerto` - He's dead).

### Hay: there is, there are

`Hay` (pronounced like "eye") comes from `haber` and means both "there is" and "there are". It never changes for plural: `Hay un banco aquí` - There's a bank here. `Hay muchos turistas` - There are lots of tourists. `¿Hay wifi?` - Is there wifi?

Use **hay** to say something exists (usually with `un/una`, numbers, or no article), and **estar** to locate something specific (with `el/la` or a name):

- `¿Hay una farmacia por aquí?` - Is there a pharmacy around here?
- `¿Dónde está la farmacia?` - Where is the pharmacy?

`Hay que` + infinitive means "one has to": `Hay que estudiar` - You (one) have to study.

> **EXERCISE:** Ser, estar or hay? (1) Mi madre ___ profesora. (2) ¿Dónde ___ los baños? (3) ___ tres libros en la mesa. (4) Hoy ___ martes. (5) Nosotros ___ cansados. (6) El concierto ___ en el estadio. (7) Tú ___ de Canadá, ¿verdad? (8) La comida ___ muy rica, gracias. (9) ¿___ un supermercado cerca? (10) Mi abuelo ___ muerto.
>
> *Answers:* (1) es. (2) están. (3) Hay. (4) es. (5) estamos. (6) es (an event). (7) eres. (8) está (it tastes delicious). (9) Hay. (10) está.

## Questions, negation and gustar

### Asking questions

Yes/no questions need no grammar change at all: just raise your intonation at the end, and write `¿...?`. Subject and verb can swap but do not have to: `¿Tienes hermanos?` - Do you have siblings? `¿Tu hermano habla inglés?` / `¿Habla inglés tu hermano?` - Does your brother speak English?

Information questions start with a question word (always accented):

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ¿Qué? | What? | ¿Qué haces? - What are you doing? |
| ¿Quién / Quiénes? | Who? | ¿Quién es? - Who is it? |
| ¿Dónde? / ¿Adónde? / ¿De dónde? | Where? / To where? / From where? | ¿Adónde vas? - Where are you going? |
| ¿Cuándo? | When? | ¿Cuándo llegas? - When do you arrive? |
| ¿Cómo? | How? | ¿Cómo se llama? - What's his/her name? |
| ¿Cuánto / Cuánta / Cuántos / Cuántas? | How much / How many? | ¿Cuántos años tienes? - How old are you? |
| ¿Por qué? | Why? | ¿Por qué estudias español? |
| ¿Cuál / Cuáles? | Which? What? | ¿Cuál es tu número? - What's your number? |

The answer to `¿por qué?` - why is `porque` - because (one word, no accent). And `¿qué?` vs `¿cuál?`: use `cuál` with `ser` when choosing among possibilities, even where English says "what": `¿Cuál es tu dirección?` - What's your address? Use `qué es` to ask for a definition: `¿Qué es un "aguacate"?`

Tag questions: add `¿verdad?` or `¿no?` - right?: `Eres de Texas, ¿verdad?`

### Negation

Put `no` directly before the verb (and before any object pronouns): `No hablo francés` - I don't speak French. `No lo sé` - I don't know (it).

Spanish **requires** double negatives, which English teachers taught you to avoid:

| Positive | Negative | Example |
|---|---|---|
| algo - something | nada - nothing | No quiero nada. - I don't want anything. |
| alguien - someone | nadie - nobody | No viene nadie. - Nobody is coming. |
| siempre - always | nunca - never | No como carne nunca. / Nunca como carne. |
| también - also | tampoco - neither | A mí tampoco. - Me neither. |
| algún / alguno - some | ningún / ninguno - no, none | No tengo ningún problema. |

When the negative word comes before the verb, you drop `no`: `Nadie viene`, `Nunca como carne`.

### Gustar and verbs like it

`Gustar` does not mean "to like". It means "to be pleasing to". The thing you like is the **subject**, and the person who likes it is an **indirect object**:

`Me gusta el chocolate.` - Chocolate is pleasing to me = I like chocolate.

So the verb agrees with the thing liked, and you almost only need two forms:

| Indirect object | Singular thing / infinitive | Plural thing |
|---|---|---|
| me - to me | me gusta el café | me gustan los perros |
| te - to you | te gusta bailar | te gustan las películas |
| le - to him/her/you (Ud.) | le gusta la música | le gustan los deportes |
| nos - to us | nos gusta viajar | nos gustan los tacos |
| os - to you all (Spain) | os gusta el fútbol | os gustan las fiestas |
| les - to them / you all | les gusta cocinar | les gustan los gatos |

Add `a + person` for emphasis or clarity (with le/les you often need it): `A mí me gusta, pero a mi hermano no le gusta` - I like it, but my brother doesn't. `A Ana le gustan las novelas` - Ana likes novels. Short replies: `A mí también` - Me too. `A mí tampoco` - Me neither. `A mí no` - Not me.

Verbs that work the same way: `encantar` - to love (things): `Me encanta esta canción`, `interesar` - to interest, `importar` - to matter, `molestar` - to bother, `doler (ue)` - to hurt: `Me duele la cabeza` - My head hurts, `faltar` - to be missing, `parecer` - to seem: `¿Qué te parece?` - What do you think?

> **NOTE:** For people, `gustar` implies attraction. To say you like someone as a person, use `Me cae bien` - I like him/her (lit. he falls well to me). `Te quiero` - I love you is used for family, partners and close friends; `te amo` is more intense and romantic.

> **EXERCISE:** Translate: (1) I like movies. (2) She likes to read. (3) We don't like cold coffee. (4) Do you (tú) like my shoes? (5) My feet hurt. (6) Nobody understands anything. (7) Why are you studying Spanish? Because I like it. (8) Which is your favourite band?
>
> *Answers:* (1) Me gustan las películas. (2) (A ella) le gusta leer. (3) No nos gusta el café frío. (4) ¿Te gustan mis zapatos? (5) Me duelen los pies. (6) Nadie entiende nada. (7) ¿Por qué estudias español? Porque me gusta. (8) ¿Cuál es tu grupo (banda) favorito (favorita)?

## Routines, plans and right now

### Reflexive verbs: your daily routine

A reflexive verb is one where the subject does the action to itself. In the dictionary it appears with `-se` attached: `levantarse` - to get (oneself) up. The `se` changes to match the subject and goes before the conjugated verb:

| | levantarse - to get up |
|---|---|
| yo | me levanto |
| tú | te levantas |
| él/ella/Ud. | se levanta |
| nosotros | nos levantamos |
| vosotros | os levantáis |
| ellos/Uds. | se levantan |

Routine verbs: `despertarse (ie)` - to wake up, `ducharse` - to shower, `lavarse los dientes` - to brush your teeth (note `los`, not `mis`: the reflexive already says whose), `vestirse (i)` - to get dressed, `acostarse (ue)` - to go to bed, `sentarse (ie)` - to sit down, `llamarse` - to be called.

Some verbs change meaning when reflexive: `ir` - to go vs `irse` - to leave, `dormir` - to sleep vs `dormirse` - to fall asleep, `llamar` - to call vs `llamarse` - to be named.

> Me despierto a las siete, pero me levanto a las siete y cuarto. Me ducho, me visto y desayuno. Por la noche me acuesto a las once.
>
> *I wake up at seven, but I get up at a quarter past. I shower, get dressed and have breakfast. At night I go to bed at eleven.*

### Ir a + infinitive: the easy future

Spanish has a simple future tense (`hablaré` - I will speak), but in conversation people mostly use `ir a` + infinitive, exactly like English "going to":

- `Voy a estudiar esta noche.` - I'm going to study tonight.
- `¿Qué vas a hacer el fin de semana?` - What are you going to do this weekend?
- `Vamos a ver.` - Let's see. (`vamos a` + infinitive also means "let's")
- `Va a llover.` - It's going to rain.

Combine with time words: `mañana` - tomorrow, `pasado mañana` - the day after tomorrow, `esta tarde` - this afternoon, `el próximo año` / `el año que viene` - next year.

For polite wishes, learn `me gustaría` - I would like as a chunk: `Me gustaría viajar a Chile` - I'd like to travel to Chile.

### The present progressive: happening right now

`estar` + **gerund** (-ndo form) describes an action in progress at this moment:

| Verb type | Gerund | Example |
|---|---|---|
| -ar | -ando: hablando | Estoy hablando por teléfono. - I'm talking on the phone. |
| -er / -ir | -iendo: comiendo, viviendo | ¿Qué estás haciendo? - What are you doing? |
| vowel + -er/-ir | -yendo: leyendo, oyendo, trayendo | Está leyendo. - She's reading. |
| e -> i / o -> u stems | pidiendo, diciendo, durmiendo | El bebé está durmiendo. |

> **WARNING:** Spanish uses the progressive much less than English. It never describes the future (`Mañana salgo` - I'm leaving tomorrow, not `estoy saliendo mañana`) and is rarely used for general states: `Llevo una camisa azul` - I'm wearing a blue shirt is more natural than the progressive. When in doubt, use the simple present.

> **EXERCISE:** (1) Describe your morning in four sentences using reflexive verbs. (2) Translate: "Tomorrow we're going to eat at my grandmother's house." (3) Translate: "What are you doing? - I'm reading a book." (4) Put in the right form: Mis hermanos (acostarse) tarde.
>
> *Answers:* (1) Sample: Me despierto a las seis y media. Me levanto enseguida. Me ducho y me visto. Salgo de casa a las siete y media. (2) Mañana vamos a comer en casa de mi abuela. (3) ¿Qué estás haciendo? - Estoy leyendo un libro. (4) se acuestan.

## Telling stories in the past

Spanish has two simple past tenses, and the difference between them is less about *when* something happened and more about *how you are looking at it*. This is the biggest conceptual jump in the guide, so take it slowly.

### The preterite: completed events

The preterite presents an action as a completed whole, a dot on the timeline: it happened, it finished, the story moves on.

| | hablar | comer | vivir |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | hablé | comí | viví |
| tú | hablaste | comiste | viviste |
| él/ella/Ud. | habló | comió | vivió |
| nosotros | hablamos | comimos | vivimos |
| vosotros | hablasteis | comisteis | vivisteis |
| ellos/Uds. | hablaron | comieron | vivieron |

The accents matter: `hablo` - I speak, `habló` - he spoke. Also note that `hablamos` and `vivimos` are identical in present and preterite; context decides.

Spelling changes in the yo form keep the sound constant: `buscar -> busqué`, `llegar -> llegué`, `empezar -> empecé`. Verbs like `leer` use y: `leyó, leyeron`. And `-ir` boot verbs change in the third person: `pedir -> pidió, pidieron`, `dormir -> durmió, durmieron`.

The key irregulars share their own endings (-e, -iste, -o, -imos, -isteis, -ieron) with no accents:

| Infinitive | Preterite (yo, tú, él, nosotros, ellos) |
|---|---|
| ser / ir (identical!) | fui, fuiste, fue, fuimos, fueron |
| estar | estuve, estuviste, estuvo, estuvimos, estuvieron |
| tener | tuve, tuviste, tuvo, tuvimos, tuvieron |
| hacer | hice, hiciste, hizo, hicimos, hicieron |
| poder | pude, pudiste, pudo, pudimos, pudieron |
| querer | quise, quisiste, quiso, quisimos, quisieron |
| venir | vine, viniste, vino, vinimos, vinieron |
| decir | dije, dijiste, dijo, dijimos, dijeron |
| saber | supe, supiste, supo, supimos, supieron |
| dar | di, diste, dio, dimos, dieron |
| ver | vi, viste, vio, vimos, vieron |

`Fui` means both "I went" and "I was"; context always makes it clear: `Fui a Cancún` - I went to Cancún, `Fue increíble` - It was incredible.

### The imperfect: background, habits, description

The imperfect presents an action as ongoing, without a clear beginning or end: the scenery, the "used to", the "was doing". It is the most regular tense in Spanish, with only three irregular verbs.

| | hablar | comer / vivir | ser | ir | ver |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| yo | hablaba | comía | era | iba | veía |
| tú | hablabas | comías | eras | ibas | veías |
| él/ella/Ud. | hablaba | comía | era | iba | veía |
| nosotros | hablábamos | comíamos | éramos | íbamos | veíamos |
| vosotros | hablabais | comíais | erais | ibais | veíais |
| ellos/Uds. | hablaban | comían | eran | iban | veían |

The yo and él forms are identical, so add the subject when it is ambiguous. And `había` - there was/were is the imperfect of `hay`.

### Choosing between them

![Timeline with the imperfect as an ongoing band and the preterite as points, plus trigger words for each](study/spanish-zero-to-conversational/preterite-imperfect.svg "The imperfect is the video in the background; the preterite is the snapshots that move the story")

Think of telling a story as making a film. The imperfect is the **camera panning over the scene**: what the weather was like, what time it was, what people were doing, how they felt, what always used to happen. The preterite is the **action**: the events, one after another, that make the plot advance.

`Era un sábado. Hacía sol y yo leía en el parque. De repente, un perro me robó el sándwich. Corrí detrás de él, pero no lo encontré.`
*It was a Saturday. It was sunny and I was reading in the park. Suddenly, a dog stole my sandwich. I ran after it, but I didn't find it.*

| Use the preterite for | Use the imperfect for |
|---|---|
| completed single events: `Llamé a mi madre.` | ongoing actions: `Llovía mucho.` |
| a series of events: `Me levanté, me duché y salí.` | habits, "used to": `De niño jugaba al fútbol.` |
| a specific, bounded time: `Viví en Lima dos años.` | description, age, time: `Tenía diez años. Eran las ocho.` |
| an interruption: `...cuando sonó el teléfono.` | the action that was interrupted: `Dormía cuando...` |
| triggers: ayer, anoche, el año pasado, una vez, de repente, en 2019, el lunes | triggers: siempre, todos los días, a menudo, de niño, cuando era pequeño, mientras, los lunes |

Note `Viví en Lima dos años`: the two years are a closed, bounded block, so preterite, even though it lasted a while. Length does not decide; boundedness does.

A few verbs shift meaning between tenses: `conocí a Ana` - I met Ana (the moment knowing began) vs `conocía a Ana` - I knew Ana. `Supe la verdad` - I found out the truth vs `sabía la verdad` - I knew the truth. `No pude` - I couldn't (tried and failed) vs `no podía` - I wasn't able to (general).

### A bonus tense: the present perfect

`haber` + past participle = "have done". Participles: `-ar -> -ado` (hablado), `-er/-ir -> -ido` (comido, vivido). Forms of haber: `he, has, ha, hemos, habéis, han`.

- `¿Has estado en México?` - Have you been to Mexico?
- `Nunca he visto la nieve.` - I've never seen snow.
- `Todavía no he terminado.` - I haven't finished yet.

Irregular participles: `hecho` - done, `dicho` - said, `visto` - seen, `escrito` - written, `puesto` - put, `vuelto` - returned, `abierto` - opened, `roto` - broken, `muerto` - dead. As mentioned earlier, Spain also uses this tense for anything that happened "today".

> **EXERCISE:** Preterite or imperfect? (1) Cuando (ser) niña, (vivir) en Ohio. (2) Ayer (ir) al cine con mis amigos. (3) (Hacer) frío y (nevar) cuando (llegar) al aeropuerto (yo). (4) Todos los veranos mi familia (ir) a la playa. (5) Anoche no (poder, yo) dormir. (6) Mientras yo (cocinar), mi hermano (poner) la mesa.
>
> *Answers:* (1) era, vivía. (2) fui. (3) Hacía, nevaba, llegué. (4) iba. (5) pude. (6) cocinaba, ponía (two simultaneous background actions).

## Pronouns, por and para

### Direct and indirect object pronouns

A **direct object** receives the action (what? whom?): I see *the house*. An **indirect object** is the person who receives it or benefits (to/for whom?): I give *my mom* the flowers.

![Pronoun table and a flow showing placement before conjugated verbs, attached to infinitives, gerunds and commands](study/spanish-zero-to-conversational/object-pronouns.svg "Object pronouns: before a conjugated verb, optionally attached to infinitives and -ndo, attached to affirmative commands")

| Person | Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|---|
| yo | me | me |
| tú | te | te |
| él / ella / usted | lo / la | le |
| nosotros | nos | nos |
| vosotros | os | os |
| ellos / ellas / ustedes | los / las | les |

- `¿Ves el coche? - Sí, lo veo.` - Do you see the car? Yes, I see it.
- `¿Tienes la llave? - No, no la tengo.` - Do you have the key? No, I don't have it.
- `Le escribo a mi abuela.` - I write to my grandmother. (Spanish often keeps both `le` and `a mi abuela`; this redundancy is normal.)

> **NOTE:** In central and northern Spain, many speakers use `le` instead of `lo` for a male person as direct object (`le veo` - I see him). This is called **leísmo** and is accepted by the RAE for masculine singular people. Recognize it, but `lo` is correct everywhere.

### Where the pronouns go

- **Before a conjugated verb:** `Lo compro.` `No te entiendo.` `Me han llamado.`
- **With an infinitive or gerund**, either before the conjugated verb or attached to the end: `Lo voy a comprar` = `Voy a comprarlo`. `Lo estoy leyendo` = `Estoy leyéndolo`. Both are equally correct.
- **With an affirmative command**, always attached: `¡Cómpralo!` - Buy it! **With a negative command**, before: `¡No lo compres!`

### Two pronouns together

Order is always **indirect before direct** ("ID"): `Me lo das` - You give it to me. And when `le` or `les` meets `lo, la, los, las`, the first becomes `se`, because `le lo` is not allowed:

- `Le doy el libro a Juan.` -> `Se lo doy.` - I give it to him.
- `¿Les mandas las fotos? - Sí, se las mando ahora.` - Are you sending them the photos? Yes, I'm sending them to them now.

Attaching pronouns can push the stress back beyond the next-to-last syllable, so add a written accent to keep it where it was: `da` + `me` + `lo` = `dámelo`, `comprando` + `lo` = `comprándolo`, `decir` + `me` + `lo` = `decírmelo`.

> **EXERCISE:** Replace the underlined objects with pronouns. (1) Compro *el pan*. (2) Voy a llamar *a mis padres* (two ways). (3) Doy *el regalo* *a mi hermana*. (4) ¡Escribe *la carta*! (5) No comas *las galletas*. (6) Estoy haciendo *la tarea* (two ways).
>
> *Answers:* (1) Lo compro. (2) Los voy a llamar / Voy a llamarlos. (3) Se lo doy. (4) ¡Escríbela! (5) No las comas. (6) La estoy haciendo / Estoy haciéndola.

### Por vs para

Both translate as "for" (and sometimes "by", "through", "in order to"), which is why they are confusing. The picture that helps: **por looks back** at the cause, route or exchange behind an action; **para looks ahead** to the goal, destination or deadline in front of it.

![An action with por pointing back to cause and route, para pointing ahead to goal and deadline, with six uses each](study/spanish-zero-to-conversational/por-vs-para.svg "Por looks back (why, through, in exchange); para looks ahead (what for, to where, by when)")

| POR | Example | PARA | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| cause, reason | Gracias por la ayuda. - Thanks for the help. | purpose (+ infinitive) | Estudio para aprender. - I study (in order) to learn. |
| through, along, around | Paseamos por el parque. - We walked through the park. | destination | Salgo para Madrid. - I'm leaving for Madrid. |
| exchange, price | Lo compré por 10 euros. - I bought it for 10 euros. | deadline | Es para el viernes. - It's due Friday. |
| duration | Estudié por dos horas. - I studied for two hours. | recipient | Este regalo es para ti. - This gift is for you. |
| means | Hablamos por teléfono. - We talked on the phone. | opinion | Para mí, es fácil. - For me, it's easy. |
| rate, per | tres veces por semana - three times a week | employer | Trabaja para mi tío. - She works for my uncle. |

For duration, many speakers simply drop the preposition (`Estudié dos horas`) or use `durante`; `por` is still correct. Fixed expressions with por are worth learning as vocabulary: `por favor` - please, `por fin` - finally, `por eso` - that's why, `por supuesto` - of course, `por ejemplo` - for example, `por cierto` - by the way, `por la mañana` - in the morning.

A minimal pair that shows the logic: `Lo hice por ti` - I did it because of you / on your behalf, vs `Lo hice para ti` - I made it for you (you are the recipient).

> **EXERCISE:** Por or para? (1) Salimos ___ la playa a las ocho. (2) Gracias ___ todo. (3) Necesito el informe ___ mañana. (4) Caminamos ___ la ciudad. (5) Estudio ___ ser médica. (6) Cambié mi bici ___ una guitarra. (7) ___ mí, el español es más fácil que el francés. (8) Te llamo ___ teléfono.
>
> *Answers:* (1) para. (2) por. (3) para. (4) por. (5) para. (6) por. (7) Para. (8) por.

## Commands and a first taste of the subjunctive

### Commands (the imperative)

Commands let you give instructions, directions and advice. The forms you need first:

| | Affirmative | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| tú, regular | = él form: ¡Habla! ¡Come! ¡Escribe! | no + subjunctive: ¡No hables! ¡No comas! ¡No escribas! |
| usted | "opposite vowel": ¡Hable! ¡Coma! ¡Escriba! | ¡No hable! ¡No coma! |
| ustedes | ¡Hablen! ¡Coman! | ¡No hablen! ¡No coman! |
| nosotros (let's) | ¡Hablemos! or ¡Vamos a hablar! | ¡No hablemos! |

Eight irregular affirmative tú commands, best memorized as a chant: `di` (decir) - say, `haz` (hacer) - do, `ve` (ir) - go, `pon` (poner) - put, `sal` (salir) - leave, `sé` (ser) - be, `ten` (tener) - have, `ven` (venir) - come. Their negatives are regular subjunctive forms: `no digas, no hagas, no vayas, no pongas, no salgas, no seas, no tengas, no vengas`.

For usted, start from the yo form, drop the -o and switch the vowel: `tengo -> tenga`, `hago -> haga`, `digo -> diga` (which is why Spaniards answer the phone with `¿Diga?`). The irregular usted forms are `sea` (ser), `vaya` (ir), `esté` (estar), `sepa` (saber), `dé` (dar).

Useful everyday commands: `Mira` / `Mire` - look (also "listen, ..." to get attention), `Oye` / `Oiga` - hey, listen, `Espera` - wait, `Siga recto` - go straight on, `Gire a la derecha` - turn right, `Pase` - come in, `Tranquilo` - relax.

> **TIP:** In real life, polite requests often avoid commands altogether: `¿Me pasas la sal?` - Will you pass me the salt? `¿Me puede ayudar?` - Can you help me? `¿Me da un café, por favor?` - Could I have a coffee? A question in the present tense is softer than an imperative.

### The present subjunctive: an introduction

The subjunctive is not a tense but a **mood**: a set of verb forms used when the speaker is not asserting a fact but wanting, hoping, doubting, or reacting. English has traces of it ("I insist that he *be* there"). In Spanish it is everywhere, and a B1 speaker needs the most common triggers.

You already know how to form it, because negative tú and usted commands *are* subjunctive forms. Take the yo form of the present, drop -o, and swap the vowel (-ar verbs take -e endings, -er/-ir verbs take -a endings):

| | hablar | comer | vivir | tener (tengo) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| yo | hable | coma | viva | tenga |
| tú | hables | comas | vivas | tengas |
| él/ella/Ud. | hable | coma | viva | tenga |
| nosotros | hablemos | comamos | vivamos | tengamos |
| vosotros | habléis | comáis | viváis | tengáis |
| ellos/Uds. | hablen | coman | vivan | tengan |

Irregulars (the "DISHES" verbs): **d**ar - dé, **i**r - vaya, **s**er - sea, **h**aber - haya, **e**star - esté, **s**aber - sepa.

The core pattern: **trigger + que + a different subject + subjunctive.**

| Trigger | Example | English |
|---|---|---|
| querer que | Quiero que vengas a mi fiesta. | I want you to come to my party. |
| esperar que | Espero que estés bien. | I hope you're well. |
| ojalá (que) | Ojalá haga sol mañana. | I hope it's sunny tomorrow / If only... |
| es importante que | Es importante que estudies. | It's important that you study. |
| pedir que | Te pido que me llames. | I'm asking you to call me. |
| para que | Te lo explico para que lo entiendas. | I'll explain it so that you understand. |
| cuando (future) | Cuando llegues, llámame. | When you arrive, call me. |
| no creo que | No creo que sea verdad. | I don't think it's true. |

The subject change matters. Same subject, use the infinitive: `Quiero ir` - I want to go. Different subject, subjunctive: `Quiero que tú vayas` - I want you to go. English "I want you to go" is a trap; `Quiero tú ir` is wrong.

`Ojalá` comes from Arabic (roughly "if God wills") and is one of the most useful words in the language: `¡Ojalá!` alone means "I hope so!"

(4) Es importante que nosotros (llegar) a tiempo. (5) Quiero que ustedes (ir) conmigo. (6) Cuando (terminar, tú), avísame. Bonus: give the affirmative and negative tú commands of *poner* and *hacer*.

## Numbers, time and everyday vocabulary

### Numbers

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 cero | 1 uno | 2 dos | 3 tres |
| 4 cuatro | 5 cinco | 6 seis | 7 siete |
| 8 ocho | 9 nueve | 10 diez | 11 once |
| 12 doce | 13 trece | 14 catorce | 15 quince |
| 16 dieciséis | 17 diecisiete | 18 dieciocho | 19 diecinueve |
| 20 veinte | 21 veintiuno | 22 veintidós | 23 veintitrés |
| 30 treinta | 31 treinta y uno | 40 cuarenta | 50 cincuenta |
| 60 sesenta | 70 setenta | 80 ochenta | 90 noventa |
| 100 cien | 101 ciento uno | 200 doscientos | 500 quinientos |
| 700 setecientos | 900 novecientos | 1000 mil | 1.000.000 un millón |

16-29 are written as one word; from 31 on you use `y`: `cuarenta y cinco`. `Cien` is exactly 100; above that it becomes `ciento`: `ciento veinte`. Hundreds agree in gender: `doscientas personas`. `Uno` shortens to `un` before masculine nouns (`veintiún años`) and becomes `una` before feminine ones. Years are read as full numbers: 2026 = `dos mil veintiséis`. Most Spanish-speaking countries write a comma for decimals and a period for thousands (1.500,50), though Mexico and several others follow the US style.

### Days, months and dates

Days and months are **not capitalized**. Days: `lunes, martes, miércoles, jueves, viernes, sábado, domingo` (the week starts on Monday). Months: `enero, febrero, marzo, abril, mayo, junio, julio, agosto, septiembre, octubre, noviembre, diciembre`.

- `¿Qué día es hoy? - Hoy es jueves.` - What day is it today? Today is Thursday.
- `el lunes` - on Monday, `los lunes` - on Mondays (no word for "on").
- Dates: day first, cardinal numbers: `el 5 de mayo de 2026`. The first of the month is `el primero` in most of Latin America and usually `el uno` in Spain.
- `¿Cuándo es tu cumpleaños? - Es el 12 de octubre.` - When is your birthday? It's October 12th.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| ¿Qué hora es? | What time is it? |
| Es la una. | It's one o'clock. (singular: one hour) |
| Son las dos. / Son las tres y cinco. | It's two. / It's 3:05. |
| Son las cuatro y cuarto. | It's a quarter past four. |

### Thematic vocabulary

**Family (la familia):** `la madre / el padre` - mother/father (`mamá, papá`), `los padres` - parents, `el hermano / la hermana` - brother/sister, `los hermanos` - siblings, `el hijo / la hija` - son/daughter, `el abuelo / la abuela` - grandfather/grandmother, `el tío / la tía` - uncle/aunt, `el primo / la prima` - cousin, `el esposo / la esposa` - husband/wife, `el novio / la novia` - boyfriend/girlfriend, `el perro / el gato` - dog/cat.

**School (el colegio, el instituto, la prepa):** `la clase` - class, `la asignatura` (Spain) / `la materia` (LatAm) - subject, `las matemáticas`, `la historia`, `las ciencias`, `la tarea` / `los deberes` (Spain) - homework, `el examen` - exam, `aprobar (ue)` - to pass, `suspender` (Spain) / `reprobar (ue)` (Mexico) - to fail, `la nota` / `la calificación` - grade, `el recreo` - break, `el/la profesor/a` or `el/la maestro/a` - teacher, `el horario` - timetable.

**Food and drink (la comida y la bebida):** `el desayuno / el almuerzo / la cena` - breakfast/lunch/dinner (in Mexico `la comida` is the main midday meal), `el pan` - bread, `el agua` - water, `la leche` - milk, `el huevo` - egg, `el pollo` - chicken, `la carne` - meat, `el pescado` - fish (as food; `el pez` is a live fish), `el arroz` - rice, `las verduras` - vegetables, `la fruta` - fruit, `el queso` - cheese, `la cuenta` - the bill, `la propina` - tip, `Tengo hambre` - I'm hungry, `¡Buen provecho!` - Enjoy your meal!

**Shopping (de compras):** `¿Cuánto cuesta? / ¿Cuánto es?` - How much is it/does it cost?, `caro / barato` - expensive/cheap, `la talla` - size (clothes), `probarse (ue)` - to try on: `¿Me lo puedo probar?` - Can I try it on?, `el probador` - fitting room, `en efectivo / con tarjeta` - in cash / by card, `la tienda` - shop, `el mercado` - market, `el recibo` - receipt, `Solo estoy mirando` - I'm just looking.

**Directions (las direcciones):** `¿Dónde está...?` - Where is...?, `¿Cómo llego a...?` - How do I get to...?, `a la derecha / a la izquierda` - to the right/left, `todo recto` (Spain) / `derecho` (LatAm) - straight ahead, `la esquina` - corner, `la cuadra` (LatAm) / `la manzana` (Spain) - block, `cerca / lejos` - near/far, `al lado de` - next to, `enfrente de` - opposite, `el semáforo` - traffic light, `la parada` - bus stop, `la estación` - station.

**Hobbies (los pasatiempos):** `jugar al fútbol` (Spain) / `jugar fútbol` (much of LatAm) - to play soccer, `tocar la guitarra` - to play the guitar (instruments use `tocar`, not `jugar`), `leer` - to read, `ver series` - to watch series, `escuchar música` - to listen to music, `ir al gimnasio` - to go to the gym, `nadar` - to swim, `bailar` - to dance, `cocinar` - to cook, `dibujar` - to draw, `salir con amigos` - to go out with friends, `los videojuegos` - video games, `En mi tiempo libre...` - In my free time...

**Weather (el tiempo, el clima):** weather uses `hacer`: `Hace calor / frío / sol / viento / buen tiempo / mal tiempo` - It's hot / cold / sunny / windy / nice / bad weather. Also `Llueve` / `Está lloviendo` - It's raining, `Nieva` - It snows, `Está nublado` - It's cloudy, `¿Qué tiempo hace?` - What's the weather like? Temperatures are in Celsius nearly everywhere: `Estamos a 30 grados` - It's 30 degrees (86 F).

### Mistakes almost every learner makes

1. **Translating "to be" with ser every time.** Run the DOCTOR/PLACE check. `Estoy cansado`, not `soy cansado`.
2. **Using estar/ser for age, hunger, cold.** `Tengo 16 años`, `tengo hambre`, `tengo frío`.
3. **"Me gusto el café."** It is `me gusta el café` (the coffee pleases), and `me gustan` for plurals.
4. **Overusing subject pronouns.** `Tengo un perro`, not `yo tengo un perro` every time.
5. **Forgetting agreement.** `Las casas blancas`, `mi amiga es alta`.
6. **English word order for pronouns.** `Lo veo`, not `veo lo`.
7. **"Quiero tú vengas."** It is `quiero que vengas`.
8. **Progressive for the future.** `Salgo mañana`, not `estoy saliendo mañana`.
9. **Pronouncing English vowels.** `Hotel` is oh-TEHL, not "hoe-tell". Say `chocolate` with four pure vowels: choh-koh-LAH-teh.
10. **Skipping the personal a.** `Visito a mis abuelos`.
11. **`Jugar` for instruments.** `Toco el piano`.
12. **"Estoy excitado" for "I'm excited".** In most contexts it suggests sexual arousal. Say `Estoy emocionado/a` or `Tengo muchas ganas`.

### Culture notes

- **Greetings are physical.** In Spain, friends and new acquaintances (except man-to-man, usually a handshake) greet with two kisses, right cheek first. In much of Latin America it is one kiss on the right cheek. In Argentina, men often greet male friends with a kiss too. Follow the local's lead.
- **Two surnames.** Most Spanish speakers carry their father's first surname followed by their mother's: Gabriel **García Márquez**. People are often called by the first surname (`el señor García`). Women generally keep their surnames after marriage.
- **Meal times.** In Spain, lunch is the big meal, around 2 to 3 p.m., and dinner is at 9 or 10 p.m. Mexico's main meal, `la comida`, is mid-afternoon too. Lingering at the table to talk after a meal is `la sobremesa`, and rushing it is rude.
- **Time is flexible socially.** A party "at nine" may start at ten. Business meetings and classes are more punctual. When unsure, just ask: `¿Es a las nueve en punto?` - Is it at nine sharp?
- **Politeness sounds different.** Spanish can sound direct to English ears: `Dame un café` at a bar in Spain is normal, not rude. Tone and `por favor` carry politeness, and so do diminutives: `un momentito` - just a moment, `un cafecito`.
- **Festivals.** Día de Muertos (Mexico, November 1-2), Semana Santa (Holy Week, everywhere), La Tomatina and San Fermín (Spain), Carnaval (many countries), and New Year's Eve with twelve grapes at midnight (`las doce uvas`, Spain and much of Latin America).
- **Hispanic, Latino, Latinx/Latine.** `Hispano` refers to Spanish-speaking heritage, `latino` to Latin American heritage. In the US, some people prefer the gender-neutral `Latinx` or `Latine`; most Spanish speakers in Latin America and Spain do not use these forms, and the Real Academia Española (RAE) does not endorse them.

> **EXERCISE:** Fix the mistakes: (1) Soy cansado porque soy 17 años. (2) Me gusto los libros. (3) Estoy embarazada porque no sé la respuesta. (4) Juego la guitarra con mi amigo. (5) Quiero tú estudias conmigo. (6) Estoy muy excitado para el viaje.
>
> *Answers:* (1) Estoy cansado porque tengo 17 años (though age is not a good reason). (2) Me gustan los libros. (3) Me da vergüenza (Estoy avergonzada) porque no sé la respuesta. (4) Toco la guitarra con mi amigo. (5) Quiero que estudies conmigo. (6) Estoy muy emocionado por el viaje / Tengo muchas ganas de hacer el viaje.

## A 16-week study plan

This plan assumes about **1.5 hours a day, six days a week** (roughly 150-170 hours), with one lighter review day. Adjust the pace, not the order.

| Week | Grammar focus | Vocabulary and skills | Speaking / output target |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sounds, stress, accents, subject pronouns | Greetings, numbers 0-20, classroom phrases | Read any text aloud; introduce yourself in 4 sentences |
| 2 | Gender, articles, plurals, adjectives | Family, colours, adjectives of personality | Describe your family |
| 3 | Regular present (-ar, -er, -ir) | School, daily activities, numbers to 100 | Talk about your week |
| 4 | Ser vs estar, hay | Places in town, feelings | First 15-minute session with a tutor or partner |
| 5 | Questions and negation, tener expressions | Food and drink | Order food in a role-play; ask 10 questions |
| 6 | Boot verbs, yo-go verbs | Time, days, months, dates | Describe your schedule |
| 7 | Gustar-type verbs, saber vs conocer | Hobbies, sports, music | Talk about likes and dislikes for 3 minutes |
| 8 | Reflexive verbs, ir a + infinitive | Daily routine, weather | Describe your routine and next weekend |
| 9 | Present progressive; review weeks 1-8 | Clothes, shopping | Shopping role-play; take an A2 practice test |
| 10 | Preterite (regular + spelling changes) | Travel, transport | Tell what you did yesterday |
| 11 | Preterite irregulars | Directions, the city | Tell a trip story; give directions |
| 12 | Imperfect; preterite vs imperfect | Childhood, descriptions | Describe your childhood (5 minutes) |
| 13 | Direct/indirect object pronouns, por vs para, present perfect | Health, body, doctor | Doctor role-play; narrate a story with pronouns |
| 14 | Commands (tú, usted) | Recipes, home, chores | Explain a recipe or give instructions |
| 15 | Present subjunctive with quiero que, espero que, ojalá | Plans, wishes, invitations | Invite someone and make plans |
| 16 | Review everything; subjunctive with para que, cuando | Opinions, current events basics | 20-30 minute conversation; take a B1 practice test |

### A typical day

| Block | Time | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Flashcards | 15 min | Anki reviews plus 10-15 new cards (sentences, not isolated words) |
| Input | 30-40 min | Graded podcast or comprehensible-input video; later, easy series with Spanish subtitles |
| Grammar | 15-20 min | The week's point: read, then do exercises, then find examples in your input |
| Output | 10-20 min | Shadowing, talking to yourself, a short journal entry, or a conversation session |

### Spaced repetition done right

- **Do reviews every day.** Anki's scheduling assumes it. Missing a week creates a painful backlog.
- **Cap new cards** at 10-20 a day. Reviews grow with every new card you add; 15 new cards a day means roughly 100-150 reviews a day after a month.
- **Make your own cards** from sentences you met in real input. They stick far better than a random premade list, because you remember the context. Premade frequency decks are fine for the first 500 words.
- **Include audio** (Forvo or the text-to-speech built into Anki) so you train your ear, not just your eyes.
- **Cloze cards** work well for grammar: `Ayer ___ (ir) al cine. -> fui`.
- **Suspend leeches.** A card you keep failing needs a better example sentence or a mnemonic, not more repetitions.

> **TIP:** Book a conversation session at the same time every week from week 4 onward. Having a fixed, slightly scary appointment is the best motivation there is, and speaking reveals your gaps faster than any test.

## Cheat sheet

```text
SOUNDS     a e i o u = ah eh ee oh oo (pure, short). h silent. j/ge/gi = throaty h.
           r = tap (pero), rr = trill (perro). ll/y = y (sh in AR/UY). ñ = ny.
           ce/ci/z = th (Spain) or s (Americas). b = v. qu = k.
STRESS     ends in vowel/n/s -> next-to-last; else -> last; otherwise write an accent.
GENDER     el -o / la -a; -ción -dad -tad -tud = fem; -ma (Greek), -aje, -or = masc.
           el día, el mapa, el problema, la mano, la foto. el agua (fem).
AGREEMENT  la casa blanca, los coches azules. Adjective after noun (mostly).
PRESENT    -ar: o as a amos áis an   -er: o es e emos éis en   -ir: o es e imos ís en
BOOT       e>ie quiero, o>ue puedo, e>i pido; never in nosotros/vosotros.
YO-GO      tengo vengo hago digo pongo salgo traigo; soy estoy voy doy sé veo conozco
SER        DOCTOR: description occupation characteristic time origin relationship; events.
ESTAR      PLACE: position location action(-ndo) condition emotion; death.
HAY        there is/are (existence). ¿Dónde está? for a specific thing.
TENER      años, hambre, sed, frío, calor, sueño, miedo, prisa, razón; tener que + inf.
GUSTAR     me/te/le/nos/os/les + gusta (singular, infinitive) / gustan (plural).
NEGATION   No + verb. Double negatives: No veo nada / nadie. Nunca voy.
FUTURE     ir a + infinitive: Voy a estudiar. Polite wish: me gustaría...
PROGRESS.  estar + -ando/-iendo (leyendo, durmiendo, pidiendo). Only for "right now".
PRETERITE  -ar: é aste ó amos asteis aron   -er/-ir: í iste ió imos isteis ieron
           fui (ser/ir), estuve, tuve, hice/hizo, pude, quise, vine, dije, supe, di, vi
IMPERFECT  -ar: aba abas aba ábamos abais aban   -er/-ir: ía ías ía íamos íais ían
           era, iba, veía. Background, habits, description, time, age.
PERFECT    he/has/ha/hemos/habéis/han + -ado/-ido (hecho dicho visto escrito puesto vuelto)
PRONOUNS   DO: me te lo/la nos os los/las   IO: me te le nos os les
           before conjugated verb; attach to inf/-ndo (optional), to + commands (always)
           IO before DO; le/les + lo/la -> se lo. Accent when attaching: dámelo.
POR        cause, through, exchange, duration, means, per. por favor/fin/eso/supuesto
PARA       purpose (+inf), destination, deadline, recipient, opinion, employer
COMMANDS   tú: habla / no hables; Ud.: hable / no hable
           di haz ve pon sal sé ten ven
```

## Where to go next

Everything below was available and free (or had a genuinely useful free tier) at the time of writing (2026). Free tiers change, so check each site.

**Courses and structured input**

- **Language Transfer - Complete Spanish** (languagetransfer.org, plus free Android and iOS apps): about 90 audio lessons that teach you to *build* sentences by thinking, not memorizing. No sign-up, no ads. Possibly the best free starting point in existence; do it alongside weeks 1-10.
- **Dreaming Spanish** (dreaming.com and YouTube): comprehensible-input videos graded from "superbeginner" to advanced, with speakers from Spain and Latin America. The free tier includes a large library (on the order of a thousand videos at the time of writing); premium adds more.
- **Coffee Break Spanish** (coffeebreaklanguages.com and any podcast app): free 15-20 minute audio lessons, a teacher and a learner working through a structured course. Paid extras exist, but the podcast itself is free.
- **Easy Spanish** (YouTube): street interviews in Spain and Latin America with Spanish and English subtitles. Great from A2 up for real accents.

**Reference and tools**

- **SpanishDict** (spanishdict.com): dictionary, full verb conjugations and grammar guides, free with ads.
- **Diccionario de la lengua española** (dle.rae.es): the Real Academia Española's official dictionary, monolingual, for when you are ready.
- **Anki / AnkiDroid** (apps.ankiweb.net): free on desktop, web and Android (the iOS app is paid). The best spaced-repetition tool available.
- **Forvo** (forvo.com): native-speaker pronunciations of individual words from many countries.

**Watching and reading**

- **RTVE Play** (rtve.es/play): Spain's public broadcaster streams many series, films and news for free, much of it with Spanish subtitles. Some content is geo-restricted outside Spain.
- Kids' and teen content in Spanish on streaming services you already have: switch audio *and* subtitles to Spanish. Series you already know in English are easiest.

**Certification and next steps**

- The **DELE** (Instituto Cervantes) and **SIELE** exams certify A1 to C2 (SIELE covers A1 to C1 in one test). A DELE A2 or B1 is a realistic goal after this guide plus a few months of practice.
- Beyond B1, the next grammar steps are the **conditional** (`hablaría`), the **simple future** (`hablaré`), the **imperfect subjunctive** (`si tuviera dinero...`) and more subjunctive triggers. But from here on, the biggest gains come from hours of listening, reading, and above all conversation. Find people, talk, make mistakes, and keep going. `¡Ánimo y mucha suerte!` - Keep going, and good luck!
